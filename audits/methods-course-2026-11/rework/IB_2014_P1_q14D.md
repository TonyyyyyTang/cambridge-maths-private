### Part (a): Polynomial solutions $P_n(x)$ of Legendre's equation

Legendre's differential equation is
\[
(1 - x^2)\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} - 2x\frac{dy}{dx} + n(n+1)y = 0, \qquad y(1) = 1,
\]
where $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$.

#### Power series solution about $x = 0$

Since $x = 0$ is an ordinary point of the ODE (as $1 - x^2 \neq 0$ at $x = 0$), we seek a power series solution
\[
y(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k x^k, \qquad y'(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k a_k x^{k-1}, \qquad y''(x) = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k(k-1) a_k x^{k-2}.
\]
Substituting into $(1 - x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y = 0$:
\[
\sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k(k-1) a_k x^{k-2} - \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k(k-1) a_k x^k - 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k a_k x^k + n(n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k x^k = 0.
\]
Shifting the index in the first sum ($k \mapsto k + 2$) and noting that $k(k-1) = 0$ for $k = 0, 1$ and $k = 0$ for $k = 0$, we combine all terms into a single series:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \Big[(k+2)(k+1) a_{k+2} + \big(n(n+1) - k(k+1)\big) a_k\Big] x^k = 0.
\]
Equating the coefficient of $x^k$ to zero for every $k \ge 0$ gives the two-step recurrence relation
\[
a_{k+2} = -\frac{n(n+1) - k(k+1)}{(k+2)(k+1)}\,a_k = -\frac{(n - k)(n + k + 1)}{(k+2)(k+1)}\,a_k \qquad (k = 0, 1, 2, \dots).
\]
This recurrence decouples the even-indexed coefficients $(a_0, a_2, a_4, \dots)$ from the odd-indexed coefficients $(a_1, a_3, a_5, \dots)$, yielding two linearly independent series solutions determined by $a_0$ (with $a_1 = 0$) and $a_1$ (with $a_0 = 0$) respectively.

#### Termination to a polynomial of degree $n$

For a non-negative integer $n$:
- When $k = n$, the numerator factor $(n - k)$ vanishes, giving $a_{n+2} = 0$, and hence by induction $a_{n+2m} = 0$ for all integers $m \ge 1$.
- For every $k$ of the same parity as $n$ with $0 \le k < n$, both $(n - k) > 0$ and $(n + k + 1) > 0$, so $a_{k+2} \neq 0$ whenever $a_k \neq 0$.

Therefore:
- If $n$ is **even**, choosing $a_0 \neq 0$ and $a_1 = 0$ produces a polynomial solution $y_n(x)$ of exact degree $n$ (containing only even powers of $x$).
- If $n$ is **odd**, choosing $a_1 \neq 0$ and $a_0 = 0$ produces a polynomial solution $y_n(x)$ of exact degree $n$ (containing only odd powers of $x$).

#### Verification that the normalisation $y(1) = 1$ can be satisfied

To rescale $y_n(x)$ to satisfy $y(1) = 1$, we must verify that $y_n(1) \neq 0$. Suppose for contradiction that a non-zero polynomial solution $y(x)$ of Legendre's equation satisfies $y(1) = 0$. Write
\[
y(x) = (x - 1)^m g(x),
\]
where $m \ge 1$ is the multiplicity of the root at $x = 1$ and $g(x)$ is a polynomial with $g(1) \neq 0$. Writing $1 - x^2 = -(x-1)(x+1)$, the lowest power of $(x - 1)$ appearing in $(1 - x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y$ is $(x - 1)^{m-1}$:
\[
(1 - x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y = \Big[-(x+1)m(m-1) - 2xm\Big] g(x)\,(x-1)^{m-1} + O\big((x-1)^m\big).
\]
Dividing by $(x - 1)^{m-1}$ and setting $x = 1$ gives
\[
\big[-2m(m-1) - 2m\big] g(1) = -2m^2 g(1) = 0.
\]
Since $m \ge 1$, this forces $g(1) = 0$, a contradiction.

Hence $y_n(1) \neq 0$. Defining
\[
P_n(x) := \frac{y_n(x)}{y_n(1)}
\]
yields a polynomial solution of exact degree $n$ satisfying $P_n(1) = 1$. (Moreover, $P_n(x)$ is the unique polynomial solution with $P_n(1) = 1$, since the difference of two such polynomials would be a polynomial solution vanishing at $x = 1$, hence identically zero by the same argument.)

#### Explicit computation of $P_0$, $P_1$, and $P_2$

Using the recurrence $a_{k+2} = -\dfrac{(n-k)(n+k+1)}{(k+2)(k+1)} a_k$:

- **$n = 0$** (even, $a_1 = 0$): $a_2 = -\frac{0 \cdot 1}{2 \cdot 1} a_0 = 0$, so $y_0(x) = a_0$. Setting $y_0(1) = 1 \implies a_0 = 1$:
  \[
  P_0(x) = 1.
  \]
- **$n = 1$** (odd, $a_0 = 0$): $a_3 = -\frac{0 \cdot 3}{3 \cdot 2} a_1 = 0$, so $y_1(x) = a_1 x$. Setting $y_1(1) = 1 \implies a_1 = 1$:
  \[
  P_1(x) = x.
  \]
- **$n = 2$** (even, $a_1 = 0$):
  \[
  a_2 = -\frac{(2 - 0)(2 + 1)}{2 \cdot 1} a_0 = -3 a_0, \qquad a_4 = -\frac{(2 - 2)(2 + 3)}{4 \cdot 3} a_2 = 0,
  \]
  so $y_2(x) = a_0(1 - 3x^2)$. Setting $y_2(1) = 1$ gives $a_0(1 - 3) = -2a_0 = 1 \implies a_0 = -\frac{1}{2}$:
  \[
  P_2(x) = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1).
  \]

### Part (b): Axisymmetric Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates

We consider
\[
\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial U}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial U}{\partial\theta}\right) = 0.
\]

#### 1. Separation of variables

Look for non-trivial separable solutions of the form $U(r, \theta) = R(r)\,\Theta(\theta)$. Substituting into Laplace's equation and multiplying by $\dfrac{r^2}{R(r)\Theta(\theta)}$ yields
\[
\frac{1}{R(r)}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \frac{1}{\Theta(\theta)\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) = 0.
\]
Since the first term is a function of $r$ alone and the second is a function of $\theta$ alone, both must equal a constant (with opposite signs):
\[
\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = \lambda, \qquad -\frac{1}{\Theta\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) = \lambda,
\]
where $\lambda$ is the separation constant.

#### 2. Angular equation and regularity at the poles $\theta = 0, \pi$

Set $x = \cos\theta \in [-1, 1]$ for $\theta \in [0, \pi]$, and write $\Theta(\theta) = y(x)$. Since $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta$ and $\sin^2\theta = 1 - x^2$, we have
\[
\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) = \frac{d}{dx}\!\left((1-x^2)\frac{dy}{dx}\right).
\]
Thus the angular ODE becomes Legendre's equation on $[-1, 1]$:
\[
(1 - x^2)\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} - 2x\frac{dy}{dx} + \lambda y = 0.
\]
Because the polar axis $\theta = 0, \pi$ ($x = \pm 1$) consists of ordinary points in $\mathbb{R}^3$, physical/smooth solutions $U(r, \theta)$ must be **regular (bounded)** as $x \to \pm 1$.

Expanding $y(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k x^k$ about $x = 0$ yields the recurrence
\[
a_{k+2} = \frac{k(k+1) - \lambda}{(k+2)(k+1)}\,a_k.
\]
For any non-terminating even or odd series, we observe
\[
\frac{(k+2)a_{k+2}}{k a_k} = 1 - \frac{\lambda}{k(k+1)}.
\]
Since $\sum \frac{|\lambda|}{k(k+1)} < \infty$, the sequence $k a_k$ converges to a finite non-zero limit $C \neq 0$ along that parity, so $a_k \sim C/k$ as $k \to \infty$. Consequently:
- A non-terminating even series behaves like $y_{\text{even}}(x) \sim -\frac{C_{\text{even}}}{2}\ln(1 - x^2)$ near $x = \pm 1$.
- A non-terminating odd series behaves like $y_{\text{odd}}(x) \sim \frac{C_{\text{odd}}}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)$ near $x = \pm 1$.

If $\lambda \neq n(n+1)$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$, neither series terminates, and for any linear combination $\alpha y_{\text{even}} + \beta y_{\text{odd}}$ to remain bounded at both $x = 1$ and $x = -1$, we require $\alpha C_{\text{even}} + \beta C_{\text{odd}} = 0$ and $\alpha C_{\text{even}} - \beta C_{\text{odd}} = 0$, forcing $\alpha = \beta = 0$.

Therefore, non-trivial solutions regular at both $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi$ exist **if and only if**
\[
\lambda = n(n+1), \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
\]
in which case the non-terminating second solution ($Q_n(x)$) is discarded for diverging logarithmically at $x = \pm 1$, leaving (up to a scalar multiple) the Legendre polynomials from part (a):
\[
\Theta_n(\theta) = P_n(\cos\theta).
\]

#### 3. Radial equation and general solution

With $\lambda = n(n+1)$, the radial ODE is the Euler–Cauchy equation
\[
\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) - n(n+1)R = 0 \implies r^2 R'' + 2r R' - n(n+1)R = 0 \qquad (r > 0).
\]
Trying $R(r) = r^\alpha$ gives the indicial equation
\[
\alpha(\alpha - 1) + 2\alpha - n(n+1) = \alpha^2 + \alpha - n(n+1) = (\alpha - n)(\alpha + n + 1) = 0,
\]
with roots $\alpha = n$ and $\alpha = -(n+1)$. Since $n - (-(n+1)) = 2n + 1 \ge 1 > 0$ for every $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ (including $n = 0$, where the roots are $0$ and $-1$), the two roots are always distinct. Hence
\[
R_n(r) = A_n r^n + B_n r^{-(n+1)},
\]
where $A_n, B_n$ are arbitrary constants.

By linearity and completeness of the Legendre polynomials $\{P_n(\cos\theta)\}_{n=0}^\infty$, the general axisymmetric solution of Laplace's equation regular on the polar axis $\theta = 0, \pi$ is
\[
U(r, \theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(A_n r^n + \frac{B_n}{r^{n+1}}\right) P_n(\cos\theta).
\]

#### 4. Solution satisfying the boundary conditions

We require $U(r, \theta)$ (in the exterior region $r \ge r_0 > 0$) to satisfy:
\[
\text{(i) } U(r, \theta) \to v_0\,r\,\cos\theta \quad \text{as } r \to \infty, \qquad \text{(ii) } \frac{\partial U}{\partial r} = 0 \quad \text{at } r = r_0.
\]
Using $P_0(\cos\theta) = 1$ and $P_1(\cos\theta) = \cos\theta$ from part (a), condition (i) means $U(r,\theta) - v_0 r P_1(\cos\theta) \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$:
\[
A_0 P_0(\cos\theta) + (A_1 - v_0) r P_1(\cos\theta) + \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} A_n r^n P_n(\cos\theta) + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{B_n}{r^{n+1}} P_n(\cos\theta) \to 0 \qquad (r \to \infty).
\]
Because the Legendre polynomials $\{P_n(x)\}_{n=0}^\infty$ are orthogonal on $[-1, 1]$ (with $\int_{-1}^1 P_m(x) P_n(x)\,dx = 0$ for $m \neq n$ and $> 0$ for $m = n$), projecting onto each mode $P_m(\cos\theta)$ shows that as $r \to \infty$:
- For $m \ge 2$: $A_m r^m + B_m r^{-(m+1)} \to 0 \implies A_m = 0$.
- For $m = 1$: $(A_1 - v_0) r + B_1 r^{-2} \to 0 \implies A_1 = v_0$.
- For $m = 0$: $A_0 + B_0 r^{-1} \to 0 \implies A_0 = 0$.

Thus $U(r, \theta)$ reduces to
\[
U(r, \theta) = v_0 r P_1(\cos\theta) + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{B_n}{r^{n+1}} P_n(\cos\theta).
\]
Differentiating with respect to $r$:
\[
\frac{\partial U}{\partial r}(r, \theta) = v_0 P_1(\cos\theta) - \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(n+1) B_n}{r^{n+2}} P_n(\cos\theta).
\]
Imposing condition (ii) at $r = r_0 > 0$:
\[
-\frac{B_0}{r_0^2} P_0(\cos\theta) + \left(v_0 - \frac{2 B_1}{r_0^3}\right) P_1(\cos\theta) - \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{(n+1) B_n}{r_0^{n+2}} P_n(\cos\theta) = 0 \qquad \forall \theta \in [0, \pi].
\]
Again by orthogonality of $\{P_n(\cos\theta)\}_{n=0}^\infty$ on $[0, \pi]$, every coefficient must vanish:
- $n = 0$: $-\dfrac{B_0}{r_0^2} = 0 \implies B_0 = 0$.
- $n = 1$: $v_0 - \dfrac{2 B_1}{r_0^3} = 0 \implies B_1 = \dfrac{1}{2} v_0 r_0^3$.
- $n \ge 2$: $-\dfrac{(n+1) B_n}{r_0^{n+2}} = 0 \implies B_n = 0$.

Substituting back yields the solution
\[
U(r, \theta) = v_0\left(r + \frac{r_0^3}{2 r^2}\right)\cos\theta.
\]

*(Remark: if the far-field condition $U(r,\theta) \to v_0 r\cos\theta$ were interpreted in the weaker asymptotic sense $U(r,\theta) \sim v_0 r\cos\theta$, i.e. $U(r,\theta) = v_0 r\cos\theta + o(r)$ as in potential flow where velocity $\nabla U \to v_0 \hat{\mathbf{z}}$ leaves the potential determined only up to an additive constant, then $A_0$ would remain an arbitrary constant, giving $U(r,\theta) = A_0 + v_0\left(r + \frac{r_0^3}{2r^2}\right)\cos\theta$. Under the strict limit $U(r,\theta) - v_0 r\cos\theta \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$, $A_0 = 0$ uniquely.)*
