## Full Solution

### 1. Fourier coefficient formulae and statement & proof of Parseval's theorem

#### Orthogonality relations on $(-T, T)$
For integers $m, n \geqslant 1$:
- $\displaystyle \int_{-T}^{T} 1\,dt = 2T$, $\quad\displaystyle \int_{-T}^{T}\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt = \left[\frac{T}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\right]_{-T}^{T} = 0$, $\quad\displaystyle \int_{-T}^{T}\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt = 0$ (odd integrand).
- $\displaystyle \int_{-T}^{T}\cos\frac{m\pi t}{T}\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt = 0$ (odd integrand on symmetric interval).
- Using $2\cos A\cos B = \cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)$:
  $$
  \int_{-T}^{T}\cos\frac{m\pi t}{T}\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt
  = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-T}^{T}\!\left(\cos\frac{(m - n)\pi t}{T} + \cos\frac{(m + n)\pi t}{T}\right)dt
  = T\delta_{mn}.
  $$
- Using $2\sin A\sin B = \cos(A - B) - \cos(A + B)$:
  $$
  \int_{-T}^{T}\sin\frac{m\pi t}{T}\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt
  = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-T}^{T}\!\left(\cos\frac{(m - n)\pi t}{T} - \cos\frac{(m + n)\pi t}{T}\right)dt
  = T\delta_{mn}.
  $$

#### Formulae for $a_0$, $a_n$, and $b_n$
Multiplying the Fourier series $f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T} + b_n\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\right)$ by $1$, $\cos\frac{m\pi t}{T}$, and $\sin\frac{m\pi t}{T}$ respectively, integrating over $(-T, T)$, and applying the orthogonality relations gives:
$$
a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\,dt, \qquad
a_n = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt \quad (n \geqslant 1), \qquad
b_n = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt \quad (n \geqslant 1).
$$

#### Statement of Parseval's theorem
If $f$ is a real square-integrable (e.g. piecewise continuous / $L^2$) function on $(-T, T)$ with Fourier coefficients $a_0, a_n, b_n$ given above, then
$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T}^{T} [f(t)]^2\,dt = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2),
$$
equivalently $\displaystyle \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} [f(t)]^2\,dt = \frac{a_0^2}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2)$.

#### Proof of Parseval's theorem
Let $S_N(t)$ be the $N$-th partial sum of the Fourier series:
$$
S_N(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T} + b_n\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\right).
$$
Expand the non-negative mean-square error $\int_{-T}^{T}(f(t) - S_N(t))^2\,dt$:
$$
\int_{-T}^{T} \big(f(t) - S_N(t)\big)^2\,dt
= \int_{-T}^{T} [f(t)]^2\,dt - 2\int_{-T}^{T} f(t) S_N(t)\,dt + \int_{-T}^{T} [S_N(t)]^2\,dt.
$$
Evaluating the two finite sums directly using the coefficient formulae and orthogonality relations (with no interchange of infinite sums and integrals):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-T}^{T} f(t) S_N(t)\,dt
&= \frac{a_0}{2}\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\,dt
 + \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(a_n\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt + b_n\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T}\,dt\right) \\
&= T\left(\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_n^2 + b_n^2)\right),
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-T}^{T} [S_N(t)]^2\,dt
&= \left(\frac{a_0}{2}\right)^2 (2T) + \sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_n^2 T + b_n^2 T) \\
&= T\left(\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_n^2 + b_n^2)\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, for every $N \geqslant 1$,
$$
\int_{-T}^{T} \big(f(t) - S_N(t)\big)^2\,dt
= \int_{-T}^{T} [f(t)]^2\,dt - T\left(\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_n^2 + b_n^2)\right). \qquad (*)
$$
*(In particular, since the left-hand side is $\geqslant 0$, this immediately implies Bessel's inequality $\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2) \leqslant \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T}^{T}[f(t)]^2\,dt$.)*

Furthermore, for any trigonometric polynomial $P_N(t) = \frac{\alpha_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (\alpha_n\cos\frac{n\pi t}{T} + \beta_n\sin\frac{n\pi t}{T})$ of degree $\leqslant N$, the same orthogonality computation gives
$$
\int_{-T}^{T} (f - P_N)^2\,dt = \int_{-T}^{T} (f - S_N)^2\,dt + T\left[\frac{(a_0 - \alpha_0)^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N}\big((a_n - \alpha_n)^2 + (b_n - \beta_n)^2\big)\right] \geqslant \int_{-T}^{T} (f - S_N)^2\,dt,
$$
so $S_N$ is the unique best $L^2$-approximation to $f$ among trigonometric polynomials of degree $\leqslant N$.

By completeness of the trigonometric system in $L^2(-T, T)$ (for any $\varepsilon > 0$, one can approximate $f$ in $L^2(-T, T)$ to within $\varepsilon/2$ by a continuous $2T$-periodic function $\tilde{f}$, and by Fejér's / Weierstrass's trigonometric approximation theorem approximate $\tilde{f}$ uniformly—hence in $L^2$—to within $\varepsilon/2$ by a trigonometric polynomial $P_N$; then $\|f - S_N\|_2 \leqslant \|f - P_N\|_2 < \varepsilon$), we have
$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{-T}^{T} \big(f(t) - S_N(t)\big)^2\,dt = 0.
$$
Taking $N \to \infty$ in $(*)$ and dividing by $T$ gives Parseval's theorem:
$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T}^{T} [f(t)]^2\,dt = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2).
$$

### 2. Fourier series of the square wave $g(t)$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$

Here $T = \pi$, and
$$
g(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & -\pi < t \leqslant 0, \\ 1 & 0 < t < \pi. \end{cases}
$$
Compute the Fourier coefficients:
- Constant term ($a_0$):
  $$
  a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(t)\,dt = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} 1\,dt = 1 \implies \frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}.
  $$
- Cosine coefficients ($a_n$, $n \geqslant 1$):
  $$
  a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(t)\cos(nt)\,dt = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\cos(nt)\,dt = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{\sin(nt)}{n}\right]_0^{\pi} = 0.
  $$
  *(Equivalently, $g(t) - \frac{1}{2}$ is odd on $(-\pi, \pi)\setminus\{0\}$, so all $a_n = 0$ for $n \geqslant 1$.)*
- Sine coefficients ($b_n$, $n \geqslant 1$):
  $$
  b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(t)\sin(nt)\,dt = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\sin(nt)\,dt
  = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\cos(nt)}{n}\right]_0^{\pi}
  = \frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n\pi}.
  $$
  Hence $b_n = 0$ for even $n$, and $b_{2k+1} = \dfrac{2}{(2k+1)\pi}$ for odd $n = 2k + 1$ ($k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).

Therefore, the Fourier series of $g(t)$ is
$$
g(t) \sim \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin\big((2k+1)t\big)}{2k+1}.
$$

### 3. Evaluation of $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)}$

Since $g$ is piecewise smooth on $[-\pi, \pi]$ and smooth (in fact constant, equal to $1$) in a neighbourhood of $t = \frac{\pi}{2} \in (0, \pi)$, Dirichlet's convergence theorem guarantees that the Fourier series converges at $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$ to $g(\pi/2) = 1$.

Using $\sin\!\left((2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = (-1)^k$ for all integers $k \geqslant 0$:
$$
1 = g\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)}.
$$
Rearranging gives
$$
\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)} = \frac{1}{2}
\implies \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)} = \frac{\pi}{4}.
$$

### 4. Evaluation of $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}$

Applying Parseval's theorem with $T = \pi$ to $g(t)$:
$$
\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} [g(t)]^2\,dt = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2).
$$
- Left-hand side:
  $$
  \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} [g(t)]^2\,dt = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} 1^2\,dt = 1.
  $$
- Right-hand side (with $a_0 = 1$, $a_n = 0$ for $n \geqslant 1$, $b_{2k} = 0$, $b_{2k+1} = \frac{2}{(2k+1)\pi}$):
  $$
  \frac{1^2}{2} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{(2k+1)\pi}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{4}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}.
  $$

Equating the two sides:
$$
1 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{4}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}
\implies \frac{4}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{1}{2}
\implies \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}.
$$

### 5. Sum of the Fourier series at $t = 0$, and comment

At $t = 0$, every term $\sin((2k+1)\cdot 0) = 0$, so the Fourier series sums directly to
$$
\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(0)}{2k+1} = \frac{1}{2}.
$$

**Comment:**
- The sum $\frac{1}{2}$ does **not** equal the assigned value $g(0) = 0$.
- Because $g$ has a jump discontinuity at $t = 0$ with left- and right-hand limits $g(0^-) = 0$ and $g(0^+) = 1$, by **Dirichlet's theorem** for piecewise smooth functions the Fourier series converges at $t = 0$ to the midpoint (arithmetic mean) of the one-sided limits:
  $$
  \frac{1}{2}\big(g(0^-) + g(0^+)\big) = \frac{1}{2}(0 + 1) = \frac{1}{2}.
  $$
- Conceptually, the Fourier coefficients $a_0, a_n, b_n$ are defined via integrals, which are completely insensitive to changing the value of $g$ at a single point (or any set of measure zero). Hence the Fourier series cannot distinguish $g(0) = 0$ from any other value assigned at $t = 0$, and always converges to the symmetric average $\frac{1}{2}(g(0^-) + g(0^+))$. (Similarly, convergence near $t = 0$ is only pointwise, not uniform, exhibiting the Gibbs phenomenon overshoot of $\approx 9\%$ of the jump height on either side of $t = 0$.)
