## Full Solution

Throughout, we use the Fourier transform convention specified in the question:
\[
\tilde{f}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx, \qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \tilde{f}(k)e^{ikx}\,dk.
\]

### Part (a)

We are given $f, g, h \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ with $\tilde{f}, \tilde{g}, \tilde{h} \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ such that $\tilde{h}(k) = \tilde{f}(k)\tilde{g}(k)$. By the Fourier inversion formula,
\[
h(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \tilde{h}(k)e^{ikx}\,dk = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \tilde{f}(k)\tilde{g}(k)e^{ikx}\,dk.
\]
Substituting $\tilde{f}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)e^{-iky}\,dy$ yields the iterated integral
\[
h(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)e^{-iky}\,dy\right)\tilde{g}(k)e^{ikx}\,dk.
\]
Because $f$ and $\tilde{g}$ are both absolutely integrable on $\mathbb{R}$,
\[
\int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left|f(y)\tilde{g}(k)e^{ik(x-y)}\right|\,dy\,dk = \left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(y)|\,dy\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty |\tilde{g}(k)|\,dk\right) < \infty.
\]
Hence by Fubini's theorem we may interchange the order of integration:
\[
h(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \tilde{g}(k)e^{ik(x-y)}\,dk\right)dy = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)\,g(x-y)\,dy.
\]
(Equivalently, setting $z = x - y$, $h(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x-z)\,g(z)\,dz = (f * g)(x)$.)

### Part (b)

We have $p'(x) = g(x)$ and assume the Fourier transforms $\tilde{p}(k)$ and $\tilde{g}(k)$ are well-defined (in particular $p(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$). Integrating by parts:
\[
\tilde{g}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty p'(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx = \Big[p(x)e^{-ikx}\Big]_{-\infty}^\infty - \int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x)(-ik)e^{-ikx}\,dx = ik\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx = ik\,\tilde{p}(k).
\]
(Equivalently, differentiating $p(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \tilde{p}(k)e^{ikx}\,dk$ under the integral sign gives $g(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty ik\,\tilde{p}(k)e^{ikx}\,dk$, so $\tilde{g}(k) = ik\,\tilde{p}(k)$.)
Therefore, for $k \ne 0$,
\[
\tilde{p}(k) = \frac{\tilde{g}(k)}{ik} = -\frac{i}{k}\tilde{g}(k).
\]

### Part (c)

Under our convention, the forward Fourier transform of $\delta(x - x_0)$ is $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \delta(x - x_0)e^{-ikx}\,dx = e^{-ikx_0}$, so the distributional inverse Fourier transform satisfies
\[
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ikx_0}e^{ikx}\,dk = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ik(x - x_0)}\,dk = \delta(x - x_0).
\]
Let $a > 0$. Writing $\cos(ka)$ and $\sin(ka)$ in complex exponential form:

1. **Inverse transform of $\cos(ka) = \frac{1}{2}\left(e^{ika} + e^{-ika}\right)$:**
   \[
   \mathcal{F}^{-1}[\cos(ka)](x)
   = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{ika} + e^{-ika}}{2}e^{ikx}\,dk
   = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ik(x+a)}\,dk + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ik(x-a)}\,dk\right)
   = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(x+a) + \delta(x-a)\bigr].
   \]

2. **Inverse transform of $\sin(ka) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{ika} - e^{-ika}\right)$:**
   \[
   \mathcal{F}^{-1}[\sin(ka)](x)
   = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{ika} - e^{-ika}}{2i}e^{ikx}\,dk
   = \frac{1}{2i}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ik(x+a)}\,dk - \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ik(x-a)}\,dk\right)
   = \frac{1}{2i}\bigl[\delta(x+a) - \delta(x-a)\bigr]
   = \frac{i}{2}\bigl[\delta(x-a) - \delta(x+a)\bigr].
   \]

### Part (d)

Take the spatial Fourier transform $\tilde{u}(k,t) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty u(x,t)e^{-ikx}\,dx$ of the wave equation $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ and its initial conditions $u(x,0) = f(x)$, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,0) = g(x)$.
Applying part (b) twice to the $x$-derivatives (assuming $u, \partial_x u \to 0$ as $|x|\to\infty$) gives $\widetilde{\partial_x^2 u}(k,t) = (ik)^2\tilde{u}(k,t) = -k^2\tilde{u}(k,t)$, so for each fixed $k$,
\[
\frac{\partial^2 \tilde{u}}{\partial t^2}(k,t) + k^2\tilde{u}(k,t) = 0, \qquad \tilde{u}(k,0) = \tilde{f}(k), \qquad \frac{\partial \tilde{u}}{\partial t}(k,0) = \tilde{g}(k).
\]
For $k \ne 0$, the general solution of this second-order ODE in $t$ is
\[
\tilde{u}(k,t) = A(k)\cos(kt) + B(k)\sin(kt).
\]
Imposing the initial conditions gives $A(k) = \tilde{f}(k)$ and $k B(k) = \tilde{g}(k)$, hence
\[
\tilde{u}(k,t) = \tilde{f}(k)\cos(kt) + \frac{\tilde{g}(k)}{k}\sin(kt).
\]
(At $k = 0$, $\tilde{u}_{tt}(0,t) = 0$ gives $\tilde{u}(0,t) = \tilde{f}(0) + t\tilde{g}(0)$, matching the limit $k \to 0$.)

Now let $p(x)$ be an antiderivative of $g(x)$, i.e. $p'(x) = g(x)$. By part (b), $\tilde{p}(k) = \dfrac{\tilde{g}(k)}{ik}$, so $\dfrac{\tilde{g}(k)}{k} = i\,\tilde{p}(k)$, and we can write
\[
\tilde{u}(k,t) = \underbrace{\tilde{f}(k)\cos(kt)}_{\tilde{h}_1(k)} + \underbrace{\tilde{p}(k)\bigl[i\sin(kt)\bigr]}_{\tilde{h}_2(k)}.
\]
Consider $t > 0$ (at $t = 0$, $(\star)$ reduces trivially to $u(x,0) = f(x)$; for $t < 0$, replacing $t$ by $-a$ with $a > 0$ uses evenness of $\cos$ and oddness of $\sin$ to produce the same formula). Taking the inverse Fourier transform of each product using part (a) (convolution theorem, assumed applicable as stated) and part (c) with $a = t > 0$:

1. Since $\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\cos(kt)](x) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(x+t) + \delta(x-t)\bigr]$, part (a) gives
   \[
   h_1(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)\cdot \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(x - y + t) + \delta(x - y - t)\bigr]\,dy
   = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)\bigl[\delta\bigl(y - (x+t)\bigr) + \delta\bigl(y - (x-t)\bigr)\bigr]\,dy
   = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[f(x+t) + f(x-t)\bigr].
   \]

2. Since $\mathcal{F}^{-1}[i\sin(kt)](x) = i\cdot\frac{1}{2i}\bigl[\delta(x+t) - \delta(x-t)\bigr] = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(x+t) - \delta(x-t)\bigr]$, part (a) gives
   \[
   h_2(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty p(y)\cdot \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(x - y + t) - \delta(x - y - t)\bigr]\,dy
   = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(y)\bigl[\delta\bigl(y - (x+t)\bigr) - \delta\bigl(y - (x-t)\bigr)\bigr]\,dy
   = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[p(x+t) - p(x-t)\bigr].
   \]
   Since $p'(\xi) = g(\xi)$, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives
   \[
   p(x+t) - p(x-t) = \int_{x-t}^{x+t} g(\xi)\,d\xi.
   \]

Summing $u(x,t) = h_1(x) + h_2(x)$ yields d'Alembert's solution:
\[
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[f(x+t) + f(x-t)\bigr] + \frac{1}{2}\int_{x-t}^{x+t} g(\xi)\,d\xi. \qquad (\star)
\]

### Part (e)

With $f(x) = 0$ and $g(x) = x$ for $|x| \le 1$ (and $0$ otherwise), $(\star)$ gives
\[
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{x-t}^{x+t} g(\xi)\,d\xi.
\]
The support of $g$ is $[-1, 1]$, and because $g(\xi) = \xi$ is odd on $[-1, 1]$, its total integral vanishes:
\[
\int_{-1}^{1} g(\xi)\,d\xi = \int_{-1}^{1} \xi\,d\xi = \left[\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right]_{-1}^{1} = 0.
\]
Therefore, whenever the integration interval $[x-t, x+t]$ completely contains $[-1, 1]$, we have $u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\xi\,d\xi = 0$. The containment condition $[-1, 1] \subseteq [x-t, x+t]$ is
\[
x - t \le -1 \quad \text{and} \quad x + t \ge 1
\iff x \le -1 + t \quad \text{and} \quad x \ge 1 - t.
\]
For $t > 1$, we have $1 - t < 0 < -1 + t$, so this defines the non-empty upward-opening wedge (cone)
\[
\mathcal{R} = \{(x,t) : t > 1,\ 1 - t \le x \le -1 + t\} = \{(x,t) : t > 1,\ |x| \le t - 1\},
\]
bounded by the characteristic lines $x = 1 - t$ and $x = -1 + t$, which clearly contains the ray $\{x = 0,\ t > 1\}$.

(For completeness, evaluating $u(x,t)$ across all five regions separated by the characteristics $x = \pm 1 \pm t$ for $t > 1$ gives:
\[
u(x,t) = \begin{cases}
0, & x \le -1 - t, \\
\frac{1}{4}\bigl[(x+t)^2 - 1\bigr] < 0, & -1 - t < x < 1 - t, \\
0, & 1 - t \le x \le t - 1, \\
\frac{1}{4}\bigl[1 - (x-t)^2\bigr] > 0, & t - 1 < x < t + 1, \\
0, & x \ge t + 1.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus $\mathcal{R}$ is precisely the maximal connected zero region for $t > 1$ containing the line $x = 0$.)

**Physical interpretation:**
The string (or 1D wave medium, with wave speed $c = 1$) starts from zero displacement with a localised initial velocity on $[-1, 1]$ that is antisymmetric ($g(-x) = -g(x)$) and therefore imparts **zero net momentum / impulse** ($\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(\xi)\,d\xi = 0$). The initial disturbance splits into two compactly supported pulses of width $2$ travelling in opposite directions at unit speed: a downward (negative) pulse moving left on $-1 - t < x < 1 - t$, and an equal-and-opposite upward (positive) pulse moving right on $t - 1 < x < t + 1$. By time $t = 1$, the trailing edges $x = 1 - t$ (of the left-moving pulse) and $x = -1 + t$ (of the right-moving pulse) have passed $x = 0$ and completely separated. Whereas a general 1D initial velocity with $\int g \ne 0$ leaves a permanent non-zero constant wake $u = \frac{1}{2}\int g(\xi)\,d\xi$ behind both pulses, here the opposing halves of the initial impulse cancel completely, so for $t > 1$ the entire wake region $|x| \le t - 1$ between the departing pulses returns to rest at $u = 0$.

## Final Answers

- **(a)** $h(x) = \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)\,g(x-y)\,dy = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x-z)\,g(z)\,dz$.
- **(b)** $\tilde{p}(k) = \dfrac{\tilde{g}(k)}{ik} = -\dfrac{i}{k}\tilde{g}(k)$ (for $k \ne 0$).
- **(c)**
  \[
  \mathcal{F}^{-1}[\cos(ka)](x) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(x+a) + \delta(x-a)\bigr], \qquad
  \mathcal{F}^{-1}[\sin(ka)](x) = \frac{1}{2i}\bigl[\delta(x+a) - \delta(x-a)\bigr] = \frac{i}{2}\bigl[\delta(x-a) - \delta(x+a)\bigr].
  \]
- **(d)** D'Alembert's solution $(\star)$ constructed via Fourier transformation of the PDE, part (b) to rewrite $\tilde{g}(k)/k = i\tilde{p}(k)$, and parts (a) and (c) to invert $\tilde{f}(k)\cos(kt) + \tilde{p}(k)[i\sin(kt)]$.
- **(e)** For $t > 1$, the region including $x = 0$ where $u(x,t) = 0$ is the wedge
  \[
  1 - t \le x \le t - 1 \qquad (\text{i.e. } |x| \le t - 1,\ t > 1),
  \]
  bounded by the lines $x = 1 - t$ and $x = -1 + t$. Physically, the left- and right-moving pulses of speed $c = 1$ have completely separated past $x = 0$ by $t = 1$, and because the antisymmetric initial velocity has zero net momentum ($\int_{-1}^1 g(\xi)\,d\xi = 0$), no residual wake displacement remains between them.
