## Full Solution

### 1. Derivation of the Euler–Lagrange equation

Consider the functional
$$
I[g] = \int_a^b f(r, g(r), g'(r))\,dr,
$$
where $f(r, g, g')$ is assumed $C^2$ in its arguments and $g \in C^2([a, b])$ satisfies fixed boundary conditions $g(a) = g_a$, $g(b) = g_b$.

Take a one-parameter family of admissible variations
$$
g_\varepsilon(r) = g(r) + \varepsilon\eta(r),
$$
where $\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\eta \in C^1([a, b])$ is an arbitrary test function satisfying the homogeneous boundary conditions
$$
\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0.
$$
Define $F(\varepsilon) = I[g + \varepsilon\eta]$. For $I$ to be stationary at $g$, we require $F'(0) = 0$ for every such $\eta$. Differentiating under the integral sign gives
$$
0 = F'(0) = \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\int_a^b f(r, g + \varepsilon\eta, g' + \varepsilon\eta')\,dr
= \int_a^b \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g}\eta + \frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\eta'\right) dr.
$$
Integrating the second term by parts,
$$
\int_a^b \frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\eta'\,dr
= \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\eta\right]_a^b - \int_a^b \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\right)\eta\,dr
= -\int_a^b \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\right)\eta\,dr,
$$
where the boundary term vanishes because $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$. Hence
$$
\int_a^b \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial g} - \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\right)\right]\eta(r)\,dr = 0
$$
for all $\eta \in C^1([a, b])$ with $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$.

By the Fundamental Lemma of the Calculus of Variations, the continuous bracketed expression must vanish identically on $[a, b]$. (Proof: if $h(r) := \frac{\partial f}{\partial g} - \frac{d}{dr}(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'})$ were nonzero at some $r_0 \in (a,b)$, say $h(r_0) > 0$, then by continuity $h(r) > 0$ on some $[r_0-\delta, r_0+\delta] \subset (a,b)$. Choosing $\eta(r) = (r - (r_0-\delta))^2((r_0+\delta) - r)^2$ on $[r_0-\delta, r_0+\delta]$ and $\eta(r) = 0$ elsewhere gives $\eta \in C^1([a,b])$ with $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$ and $\int_a^b h(r)\eta(r)\,dr > 0$, a contradiction; by continuity $h \equiv 0$ on $[a,b]$.)

Thus $g(r)$ satisfies the **Euler–Lagrange equation**:
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial g} - \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\right) = 0.
$$

### 2. Three-dimensional electrostatics energy functional and equation (1)

The energy functional is
$$
\mathcal{E}[\phi] = 2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2\right]r^2\,dr = \int_{R_1}^{R_2} f(r, \phi, \phi')\,dr,
$$
with integrand
$$
f(r, \phi, \phi') = \pi r^2\left((\phi')^2 + \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi^2\right),
$$
where $\lambda \neq 0$ (implicitly $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ in electrostatics, where $\lambda$ is the screening length). Computing the partial derivatives:
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi} = \frac{2\pi r^2}{\lambda^2}\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi'} = 2\pi r^2 \frac{d\phi}{dr}.
$$
Substituting into the Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi} - \frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi'}\right) = 0$ yields
$$
\frac{2\pi r^2}{\lambda^2}\phi - \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(2\pi r^2\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = 0.
$$
Dividing by $-2\pi r^2$ (for $r > 0$, on $0 < R_1 \leqslant r \leqslant R_2$):
$$
\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi = 0.
$$
Now expand both $\frac{d}{dr}(r^2\phi')$ and $\frac{d^2(r\phi)}{dr^2}$:
$$
\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = r^2\frac{d^2\phi}{dr^2} + 2r\frac{d\phi}{dr},
$$
and
$$
\frac{d^2(r\phi)}{dr^2} = \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\phi + r\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = 2\frac{d\phi}{dr} + r\frac{d^2\phi}{dr^2}.
$$
Hence for $r > 0$,
$$
\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = \frac{d^2\phi}{dr^2} + \frac{2}{r}\frac{d\phi}{dr} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d^2(r\phi)}{dr^2},
$$
so the Euler–Lagrange equation is equivalent to
$$
\frac{1}{r}\frac{d^2(r\phi)}{dr^2} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi = 0. \qquad (1)
$$

*Verification that this stationary point minimizes $\mathcal{E}$:* For any admissible perturbation $\phi + \eta$ with $\eta(R_1) = \eta(R_2) = 0$ and real $\lambda \neq 0$,
$$
\mathcal{E}[\phi + \eta] - \mathcal{E}[\phi]
= 2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\!\left(\phi'\eta' + \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi\eta\right)r^2\,dr
+ 2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\!\left[\frac{1}{2}(\eta')^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\eta^2\right]r^2\,dr.
$$
Integrating the linear term by parts eliminates it when $\phi$ satisfies (1), leaving $\mathcal{E}[\phi+\eta] - \mathcal{E}[\phi] = \mathcal{E}[\eta] > 0$ for every $\eta \not\equiv 0$. Thus the solution of (1) is the unique global minimizer of $\mathcal{E}$.

### 3. General solution and boundary-value solution on $R_1 \leqslant r \leqslant R_2$

Multiplying (1) by $r$ and setting $u(r) = r\phi(r)$ gives the constant-coefficient linear ODE
$$
\frac{d^2 u}{dr^2} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}u = 0.
$$
Its characteristic equation is $m^2 - 1/\lambda^2 = 0$, with roots $m = \pm 1/\lambda$ (for $\lambda \neq 0$). Hence
$$
u(r) = A e^{r/\lambda} + B e^{-r/\lambda} = C\sinh\!\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right) + D\cosh\!\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right),
$$
so the **general solution** of (1) is
$$
\phi(r) = \frac{A e^{r/\lambda} + B e^{-r/\lambda}}{r}
\qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad
\phi(r) = \frac{C\sinh(r/\lambda) + D\cosh(r/\lambda)}{r},
$$
where $A, B$ (or $C, D$) are arbitrary constants.

Now impose the boundary conditions $\phi(R_1) = \phi_1$ and $\phi(R_2) = 0$ on $0 < R_1 \leqslant r \leqslant R_2$ (with $R_1 < R_2$ and real $\lambda \neq 0$). Writing $u(r) = r\phi(r)$ in the shifted hyperbolic basis $\sinh((R_2 - r)/\lambda)$ and $\cosh((R_2 - r)/\lambda)$:
$$
r\phi(r) = K_1\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - r}{\lambda}\right) + K_2\cosh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - r}{\lambda}\right).
$$
- Applying $\phi(R_2) = 0$ gives $0 = R_2\phi(R_2) = K_2\cosh(0) = K_2$, so $K_2 = 0$.
- Applying $\phi(R_1) = \phi_1$ gives $R_1\phi_1 = K_1\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - R_1}{\lambda}\right)$. Since $R_2 > R_1$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, $\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - R_1}{\lambda}\right) \neq 0$, so
  $$
  K_1 = \frac{R_1\phi_1}{\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - R_1}{\lambda}\right)}.
  $$

Therefore, the unique solution satisfying $\phi(R_1) = \phi_1$ and $\phi(R_2) = 0$ is
$$
\phi(r) = \phi_1\frac{R_1}{r}\frac{\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - r}{\lambda}\right)}{\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - R_1}{\lambda}\right)}
= \phi_1\frac{R_1}{r}\frac{e^{(R_2 - r)/\lambda} - e^{-(R_2 - r)/\lambda}}{e^{(R_2 - R_1)/\lambda} - e^{-(R_2 - R_1)/\lambda}}.
$$
*(Note: this expression is invariant under $\lambda \mapsto -\lambda$. In the limit $R_2 \to \infty$ with $\lambda > 0$ it reduces to the Yukawa potential $\phi_1\frac{R_1}{r}e^{-(r-R_1)/\lambda}$, and in the unscreened limit $\lambda \to \infty$ it reduces to the harmonic spherical capacitor potential $\phi_1\frac{R_1(R_2-r)}{r(R_2-R_1)}$.)*

### 4. Two-dimensional annular region in cylindrical polar coordinates

In two-dimensional polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ — equivalently cylindrical polar coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ per unit length in $z$ — a radial potential $\phi = \phi(r)$ has $|\nabla\phi|^2 = (d\phi/dr)^2$, and the area element is $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$. Integrating the energy density $\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2$ over the annulus $R_1 \leqslant r \leqslant R_2$, $0 \leqslant \theta < 2\pi$ gives
$$
\mathcal{E}_{2\text{D}}[\phi]
= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\!\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2\right]r\,dr\,d\theta
= 2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\!\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2\right]r\,dr.
$$
*(Prefactor remark: In 3D spherical polars, $\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta = 4\pi$, whereas the printed 3D functional has prefactor $2\pi$, i.e. an extra factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ relative to $\int_V\left[\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2\right]dV$. If that same overall factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ is carried over literally to 2D, the prefactor is $\pi$ instead of $2\pi$; and for a finite cylinder of height $L$ in 3D cylindrical polars rather than energy per unit length, the integral is multiplied by $L$. Because any nonzero constant prefactor $C$ cancels from the Euler–Lagrange equation, the governing ODE is unaffected.)*

Taking the integrand $f_{2\text{D}}(r, \phi, \phi') = C r\left[\frac{1}{2}(\phi')^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2\right]$ (with $C = 2\pi$, or $\pi$ under the literal half-prefactor convention):
$$
\frac{\partial f_{2\text{D}}}{\partial \phi} = \frac{C r}{\lambda^2}\phi, \qquad
\frac{\partial f_{2\text{D}}}{\partial \phi'} = C r \frac{d\phi}{dr}.
$$
The Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{\partial f_{2\text{D}}}{\partial \phi} - \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f_{2\text{D}}}{\partial \phi'}\right) = 0$ is
$$
\frac{C r}{\lambda^2}\phi - \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(C r \frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = 0.
$$
Dividing by $-C r$ (for $r > 0$) yields the two-dimensional equivalent of (1):
$$
\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi = 0,
$$
equivalently
$$
\frac{d^2\phi}{dr^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{d\phi}{dr} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi = 0,
$$
which is the radial screened Poisson equation $\nabla^2\phi - \lambda^{-2}\phi = 0$ in 2D polar coordinates (the modified Bessel equation of order $0$ in $r/\lambda$).

## Final Answers

- **Euler–Lagrange equation:**
  $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial g} - \frac{d}{dr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial g'}\right) = 0.$$
- **3D general solution of (1):**

- **3D boundary-value solution ($\phi(R_1) = \phi_1$, $\phi(R_2) = 0$):**
  $$\phi(r) = \phi_1\frac{R_1}{r}\frac{\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - r}{\lambda}\right)}{\sinh\!\left(\frac{R_2 - R_1}{\lambda}\right)}.$$
- **2D annular energy functional (cylindrical polar coordinates, per unit length in $z$):**
  $$\mathcal{E}_{2\text{D}}[\phi] = 2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\!\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\phi^2\right]r\,dr$$
  *(with prefactor $\pi$ if the printed 3D functional's extra factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ relative to $4\pi$ is preserved literally)*.
- **2D equivalent of (1):**
  $$\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi = 0 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \frac{d^2\phi}{dr^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{d\phi}{dr} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\phi = 0.$$
