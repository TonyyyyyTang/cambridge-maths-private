## Full Solution

We use standard Cartesian index notation in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the Einstein summation convention over repeated indices in $\{1, 2, 3\}$.

### Part 1: Definition of a second rank tensor and scalar invariance of $M_{ii}$

**Definition.** Consider a change of orthonormal Cartesian coordinate axes in $\mathbb{R}^3$ given by $x'_i = R_{ij} x_j$ (corresponding to basis vectors $\mathbf{e}'_i = R_{ij}\mathbf{e}_j$), where $R = (R_{ij})$ is an orthogonal $3 \times 3$ matrix:
$$
R^T R = R R^T = I \iff R_{ki} R_{kj} = \delta_{ij} \text{ and } R_{ik} R_{jk} = \delta_{ij}.
$$
(For right-handed orthonormal frames, $R \in SO(3)$ has $\det R = +1$; the transformation law for a true Cartesian tensor holds for all $R \in O(3)$.)

A **second rank (Cartesian) tensor** is a set of $3^2 = 9$ components $T_{ij}$ ($i, j \in \{1, 2, 3\}$), defined with respect to every orthonormal Cartesian coordinate system, which transform under a change of axes $x'_i = R_{ij} x_j$ according to
$$
T'_{ij} = R_{ip} R_{jq} T_{pq}.
$$
A **scalar** (rank-$0$ tensor) is a single quantity $\phi$ whose value is invariant under all such changes of axes: $\phi' = \phi$.

**Proof that $M_{ii}$ is a scalar.** Let $M_{ij}$ be a second rank tensor, so that $M'_{ij} = R_{ip} R_{jq} M_{pq}$ under any orthogonal change of axes $R$. Setting $j = i$ and summing over $i = 1, 2, 3$:
$$
M'_{ii} = R_{ip} R_{iq} M_{pq}.
$$
By the orthogonality of $R$, $R_{ip} R_{iq} = (R^T)_{pi} R_{iq} = (R^T R)_{pq} = \delta_{pq}$. Therefore,
$$
M'_{ii} = \delta_{pq} M_{pq} = M_{pp} = M_{ii}.
$$
Since $M'_{ii} = M_{ii}$ in every orthonormal Cartesian frame, $M_{ii}$ is a scalar.

### Part 2: The $z$-direction is an eigenvector of $M_{ij}$ with eigenvalue $M_\perp$

The thin flat plate lies in the $(x, y)$-plane with negligible thickness in the $z$-direction, so every point $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, x_3) = (x, y, z) \in D$ satisfies $x_3 = z = 0$, and hence
$$
x_k x_k = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = x^2 + y^2 \qquad \text{on } D.
$$
Let $\mathbf{e}_3 = (0, 0, 1)$ be the unit vector in the $z$-direction, with components $(\mathbf{e}_3)_j = \delta_{j3}$. Acting with $M_{ij}$ on $\mathbf{e}_3$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{ij}(\mathbf{e}_3)_j &= M_{ij}\delta_{j3} = M_{i3} \\
&= \int_D \bigl(x_k x_k \delta_{i3} - x_i x_3\bigr)\rho\,\mathrm{d}S.
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $x_3 = 0$ identically on $D$, the term $x_i x_3$ vanishes everywhere in the integral, leaving
$$
M_{ij}(\mathbf{e}_3)_j = \delta_{i3}\int_D x_k x_k\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}S = \left(\int_D (x^2 + y^2)\,\rho(x, y)\,\mathrm{d}S\right) (\mathbf{e}_3)_i.
$$
That is, $M \mathbf{e}_3 = M_\perp \mathbf{e}_3$ (equivalently $M_{13} = M_{23} = 0$ and $M_{33} = M_\perp$). Thus the $z$-direction $\mathbf{e}_3$ is an eigenvector of $M_{ij}$ with corresponding eigenvalue
$$
M_\perp = \int_D x_k x_k\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}S = \int_D (x^2 + y^2)\,\rho(x, y)\,\mathrm{d}S.
$$

### Part 3: Perpendicular Axis Theorem $M_\perp = M_1 + M_2$

First observe that $M_{ij} = \int_D (x_k x_k \delta_{ij} - x_i x_j)\rho\,\mathrm{d}S$ is a **real symmetric** second rank tensor:
- It transforms as a second rank tensor because $x_i$ is a vector, $x_k x_k$, $\rho(\mathbf{x})$, and $\mathrm{d}S$ are scalars, and $\delta_{ij}$ is an isotropic rank-$2$ tensor;
- It is symmetric since $\delta_{ij} = \delta_{ji}$ and $x_i x_j = x_j x_i$, so $M_{ij} = M_{ji}$.

By the spectral theorem for real symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrices, there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $M_{ij}$ (principal axes), i.e., an orthogonal change of axes $R$ in which $M$ is diagonal:
$$
M' = \operatorname{diag}(M_1, M_2, M_\perp),
$$
where $M_1, M_2, M_\perp$ are the three eigenvalues of $M_{ij}$. (Since $\mathbf{e}_3$ is an eigenvector and $M$ is symmetric, the remaining two eigenvectors may be chosen orthonormal in the $(x, y)$-plane.)

Evaluating the trace in this principal-axis frame gives
$$
M'_{ii} = M_1 + M_2 + M_\perp.
$$
On the other hand, evaluating the trace directly in the original $(x, y, z)$ frame (using $\delta_{ii} = 3$ in three dimensions):
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{ii} &= \int_D \bigl(x_k x_k \delta_{ii} - x_i x_i\bigr)\rho\,\mathrm{d}S \\
&= \int_D \bigl(3 x_k x_k - x_k x_k\bigr)\rho\,\mathrm{d}S \\
&= 2\int_D x_k x_k\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}S \\
&= 2\int_D (x^2 + y^2)\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}S = 2 M_\perp.
\end{aligned}
$$
By Part 1, $M_{ii}$ is a scalar, so $M'_{ii} = M_{ii}$. Therefore,
$$
M_1 + M_2 + M_\perp = 2 M_\perp \implies M_\perp = M_1 + M_2.
$$

*(Alternative direct check: since $M_{13} = M_{31} = M_{23} = M_{32} = 0$, $M$ is block diagonal with upper-left $2\times 2$ block having eigenvalues $M_1, M_2$ and trace $M_{11} + M_{22} = \int_D y^2\rho\,\mathrm{d}S + \int_D x^2\rho\,\mathrm{d}S = \int_D (x^2 + y^2)\rho\,\mathrm{d}S = M_\perp$, hence $M_1 + M_2 = M_\perp$.)*

### Part 4: Moment of inertia tensor of a uniform circular disc

Let $D = \{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \le a^2\}$ in the $z = 0$ plane, with uniform areal density $\rho = \text{const}$ (so total mass $m = \pi a^2 \rho$).

1. **Compute $M_\perp = M_{33}$** in plane polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ with $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $\mathrm{d}S = r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta$:
   $$
   M_\perp = M_{33} = \rho\int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\int_0^a r^2\cdot r\,\mathrm{d}r = 2\pi\rho\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^a = \frac{1}{2}\pi\rho a^4.
   $$

2. **Off-diagonal components:**
   - $M_{13} = M_{31} = M_{23} = M_{32} = 0$ by Part 2 ($z = 0$ on $D$).
   - $M_{12} = M_{21} = -\rho\int_D xy\,\mathrm{d}S = -\rho\int_0^a r^3\,\mathrm{d}r\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0$ (also clear by reflection symmetry $x \mapsto -x$).

3. **Remaining diagonal components $M_{11}$ and $M_{22}$:**
   Since all off-diagonal components vanish, $M_{ij}$ is diagonal in the $(x, y, z)$ basis with eigenvalues $M_1 = M_{11}$, $M_2 = M_{22}$, and $M_\perp = M_{33}$. By rotational symmetry of the disc under a $\frac{\pi}{2}$ rotation $(x, y) \mapsto (-y, x)$ (or direct integration), $M_{11} = M_{22}$. Using Part 3 ($M_1 + M_2 = M_\perp$),
   $$
   M_{11} = M_{22} = \frac{1}{2} M_\perp = \frac{1}{4}\pi\rho a^4.
   $$
   *(Direct integration check: $M_{11} = \rho\int_0^a r^3\,\mathrm{d}r\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \rho\cdot\frac{a^4}{4}\cdot\pi = \frac{1}{4}\pi\rho a^4$, and similarly $M_{22} = \rho\int_0^a r^3\,\mathrm{d}r\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \frac{1}{4}\pi\rho a^4$.)*

Hence, in matrix form,
$$
M = \frac{\pi\rho a^4}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{m a^2}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix},
$$
or equivalently in index notation,
$$
M_{ij} = \frac{\pi\rho a^4}{4}\bigl(\delta_{ij} + \delta_{i3}\delta_{j3}\bigr) = \frac{m a^2}{4}\bigl(\delta_{ij} + \delta_{i3}\delta_{j3}\bigr),
$$
where $m = \pi a^2\rho$ is the total mass of the disc.

## Final Answers

- **Second rank tensor:** Components $T_{ij}$ transforming as $T'_{ij} = R_{ip} R_{jq} T_{pq}$ under orthogonal coordinate transformations $x'_i = R_{ij} x_j$ ($R_{ip} R_{iq} = \delta_{pq}$); hence $M'_{ii} = R_{ip} R_{iq} M_{pq} = \delta_{pq} M_{pq} = M_{ii}$ is a scalar.
- **$z$-direction eigenvalue:** $\mathbf{e}_3 = (0, 0, 1)$ is an eigenvector of $M_{ij}$ with eigenvalue
  $$
  M_\perp = \int_D (x^2 + y^2)\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}S = \int_D x_k x_k\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}S.
  $$
- **Eigenvalue identity:** $M_\perp = M_1 + M_2$ (from $M_1 + M_2 + M_\perp = M'_{ii} = M_{ii} = 2M_\perp$).
- **Uniform circular disc of radius $a$ and areal density $\rho$ (mass $m = \pi a^2\rho$) centred at the origin:**
  $$
  M = \frac{\pi\rho a^4}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{m a^2}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M_{ij} = \frac{\pi\rho a^4}{4}\bigl(\delta_{ij} + \delta_{i3}\delta_{j3}\bigr).
  $$
