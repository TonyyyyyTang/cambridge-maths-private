## Full Solution

### Preliminaries on regularity

For a **regular** Sturm–Liouville (S-L) system on the finite interval $[a, b]$ ($-\infty < a < b < \infty$), we have real-valued functions $p \in C^1[a, b]$ and $q, \omega \in C[a, b]$ with $p(x) > 0$ and $\omega(x) > 0$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, together with real boundary-condition constants $A_1, A_2, B_1, B_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfying $(A_1, A_2) \neq (0, 0)$ and $(B_1, B_2) \neq (0, 0)$.

---

### 1. Self-adjointness of $\mathcal{L}$

Consider the Hermitian inner product on $C^2[a, b]$:
\[
\langle u, v \rangle := \int_a^b u^*(x)\,v(x)\,dx.
\]
*(Equivalently, the operator $\omega^{-1}\mathcal{L}$ is self-adjoint with respect to the weighted inner product $\langle u, v \rangle_\omega := \int_a^b u^*(x)\,v(x)\,\omega(x)\,dx$, because $\langle u, \omega^{-1}\mathcal{L} v \rangle_\omega = \int_a^b u^*\,(\mathcal{L}v)\,dx = \langle u, \mathcal{L}v \rangle$.)*

Let $u, v \in C^2[a, b]$ satisfy the boundary conditions
\[
\begin{cases}
A_1 y(a) + A_2 y'(a) = 0, \\
B_1 y(b) + B_2 y'(b) = 0.
\end{cases}
\]
Since $A_1, A_2, B_1, B_2$ are real, $u^*$ satisfies the same boundary conditions as $u$:
\[
A_1 u^*(a) + A_2 (u^*)'(a) = 0, \qquad B_1 u^*(b) + B_2 (u^*)'(b) = 0.
\]
Now compute $\langle u, \mathcal{L}v \rangle$ by integrating the $-[p v']'$ term by parts twice:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle u, \mathcal{L}v \rangle &= \int_a^b u^*(x)\Big(-\big[p(x)v'(x)\big]' + q(x)v(x)\Big)\,dx \\
&= -\Big[u^*(x)\,p(x)\,v'(x)\Big]_a^b + \int_a^b \Big(p(x)\,(u^*)'(x)\,v'(x) + q(x)\,u^*(x)\,v(x)\Big)\,dx \\
&= \Big[p(x)\big((u^*)'(x)\,v(x) - u^*(x)\,v'(x)\big)\Big]_a^b + \int_a^b \Big(-\big[p(x)(u^*)'(x)\big]' + q(x)u^*(x)\Big)\,v(x)\,dx.
\end{aligned}
\]
Because $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ are real-valued, $-\big[p(u^*)'\big]' + q u^* = (\mathcal{L}u)^*$, so
\[
\langle u, \mathcal{L}v \rangle - \langle \mathcal{L}u, v \rangle = \Big[p(x)\big((u^*)'(x)\,v(x) - u^*(x)\,v'(x)\big)\Big]_a^b.
\]
At $x = a$, the boundary conditions on $u^*$ and $v$ form the homogeneous linear system
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
u^*(a) & (u^*)'(a) \\
v(a) & v'(a)
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
A_1 \\ A_2
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\ 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Because $(A_1, A_2) \neq (0, 0)$, the $2 \times 2$ matrix must be singular, so its determinant vanishes:
\[
u^*(a)v'(a) - (u^*)'(a)v(a) = 0.
\]
*(Explicitly: if $A_2 = 0$ then $A_1 \neq 0$, giving $u^*(a) = v(a) = 0$; if $A_2 \neq 0$, then $(u^*)'(a) = -\frac{A_1}{A_2}u^*(a)$ and $v'(a) = -\frac{A_1}{A_2}v(a)$; in both cases $(u^*)'(a)v(a) - u^*(a)v'(a) = 0$.)*

By the identical argument at $x = b$ using $(B_1, B_2) \neq (0, 0)$,
\[
u^*(b)v'(b) - (u^*)'(b)v(b) = 0.
\]
Thus the boundary term vanishes identically:
\[
\Big[p(x)\big((u^*)'(x)\,v(x) - u^*(x)\,v'(x)\big)\Big]_a^b = 0,
\]
and therefore $\langle u, \mathcal{L}v \rangle = \langle \mathcal{L}u, v \rangle$ for all admissible $u, v$. Hence $\mathcal{L}$ is **self-adjoint**.

### 2. Rayleigh quotient expression for $\lambda$

First observe that every eigenvalue $\lambda$ is real and may be associated with a real-valued eigenfunction $y(x)$: for any eigenfunction $y \not\equiv 0$ satisfying $\mathcal{L}y = \lambda\omega y$, self-adjointness yields
\[
\lambda \int_a^b \omega |y|^2\,dx = \langle y, \mathcal{L}y \rangle = \langle \mathcal{L}y, y \rangle = \lambda^* \int_a^b \omega |y|^2\,dx.
\]
Since $\omega(x) > 0$ on $[a,b]$ and $y \not\equiv 0$ is continuous, $\int_a^b \omega |y|^2\,dx > 0$, whence $\lambda = \lambda^* \in \mathbb{R}$. Because $p, q, \omega, \lambda, A_i, B_i$ are all real, taking real and imaginary parts of $\mathcal{L}y = \lambda\omega y$ and the boundary conditions shows that $\operatorname{Re}(y)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(y)$ are both real solutions (at least one not identically zero), so without loss of generality we may take $y(x)$ to be real-valued.

Multiplying $(\mathcal{L}y)(x) = \lambda\omega(x)y(x)$ by $y(x)$ and integrating over $[a,b]$:
\[
\lambda \int_a^b \omega(x)\,y(x)^2\,dx = \int_a^b y(x)\,(\mathcal{L}y)(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b y(x)\,\big[p(x)y'(x)\big]'\,dx + \int_a^b q(x)\,y(x)^2\,dx.
\]
Integrating the first term on the right-hand side by parts once:
\[
-\int_a^b y(x)\,\big[p(x)y'(x)\big]'\,dx = -\Big[p(x)\,y(x)\,y'(x)\Big]_a^b + \int_a^b p(x)\,{y'(x)}^2\,dx.
\]
Hence
\[
\lambda \int_a^b \omega y^2\,dx = \int_a^b \big(p{y'}^2 + qy^2\big)\,dx - \big[pyy'\big]_a^b.
\]
Since $\omega(x) > 0$ on $[a,b]$ and $y \not\equiv 0$ is continuous, $\int_a^b \omega y^2\,dx > 0$, so dividing through gives
\[
\lambda = \frac{\int_a^b \big(p{y'}^2 + qy^2\big)\,dx - [pyy']_a^b}{\int_a^b \omega y^2\,dx}.
\]

### 3. Strict positivity of eigenvalues for the specified system

Now let $a = 0$, $b = \ell$ (with $\ell > 0$), $p(x) = 1$, $q(x) \ge 0$, $\omega(x) = 1$ on $[0, \ell]$, and
\[
A_1 = 1,\; A_2 = 0 \implies y(0) = 0,
\]
\[
B_1 = k > 0,\; B_2 = 1 \implies k y(\ell) + y'(\ell) = 0, \quad \text{i.e. } y'(\ell) = -k y(\ell).
\]
Evaluate the boundary term in the Rayleigh quotient with $p(x) = 1$:
\[
-\big[p y y'\big]_0^\ell = -y(\ell)\,y'(\ell) + y(0)\,y'(0) = -y(\ell)\big(-k y(\ell)\big) + 0 = k\,y(\ell)^2.
\]
Substituting into the formula for $\lambda$ yields
\[
\lambda = \frac{\int_0^\ell \big({y'(x)}^2 + q(x)\,y(x)^2\big)\,dx + k\,y(\ell)^2}{\int_0^\ell y(x)^2\,dx}.
\]
Because ${y'(x)}^2 \ge 0$, $q(x) \ge 0$, $y(x)^2 \ge 0$, $k > 0$, and $\int_0^\ell y(x)^2\,dx > 0$, every term in the numerator is non-negative and the denominator is strictly positive, so $\lambda \ge 0$.

To prove **strict** positivity ($\lambda > 0$), suppose for contradiction that $\lambda = 0$. Then the numerator must vanish:
\[
\int_0^\ell {y'(x)}^2\,dx + \int_0^\ell q(x)\,y(x)^2\,dx + k\,y(\ell)^2 = 0.
\]
As each of the three summands is non-negative, each must vanish individually; in particular,
\[
\int_0^\ell {y'(x)}^2\,dx = 0.
\]
Since $y'$ is continuous on $[0, \ell]$ and ${y'}^2 \ge 0$, this implies $y'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in [0, \ell]$, so $y(x) = C$ is constant on $[0, \ell]$. The boundary condition $y(0) = 0$ (or equally $k y(\ell)^2 = 0$ with $k > 0$) then forces $C = 0$, so $y(x) \equiv 0$ on $[0, \ell]$, contradicting that $y$ is an eigenfunction ($y \not\equiv 0$).

Therefore every eigenvalue satisfies $\lambda > 0$.

### 4. Explicit solution when $q(x) = 0$, existence of infinitely many eigenvalues, and large-$n$ asymptotics

#### Explicit solution

Setting $q(x) = 0$, the S-L problem is
\[
-y''(x) = \lambda y(x), \qquad 0 \le x \le \ell, \qquad y(0) = 0, \qquad k y(\ell) + y'(\ell) = 0 \quad (k > 0).
\]
Since we have proved $\lambda > 0$, write $\lambda = \mu^2$ with $\mu = \sqrt{\lambda} > 0$. The general solution of $y'' + \mu^2 y = 0$ is
\[
y(x) = C \sin(\mu x) + D \cos(\mu x).
\]
Applying $y(0) = 0$ gives $D = 0$, so
\[
y(x) = C \sin(\mu x) \qquad (C \neq 0).
\]
Applying the second boundary condition $k y(\ell) + y'(\ell) = 0$:
\[
C\big(k\sin(\mu\ell) + \mu\cos(\mu\ell)\big) = 0.
\]
Since $C \neq 0$, the eigenvalue equation is
\[
k\sin(\mu\ell) + \mu\cos(\mu\ell) = 0 \qquad (\mu > 0).
\]
Observe that $\cos(\mu\ell) \neq 0$ for any root: if $\cos(\mu\ell) = 0$, then $\sin(\mu\ell) = \pm 1$, giving $k\sin(\mu\ell) + \mu\cos(\mu\ell) = \pm k \neq 0$ because $k > 0$. Dividing by $k\cos(\mu\ell) \neq 0$ yields the equivalent transcendental equation
\[
\tan(\mu\ell) = -\frac{\mu}{k}, \qquad \mu > 0.
\]
In terms of the dimensionless variable $\xi := \mu\ell > 0$ (so $\lambda = \mu^2 = \xi^2/\ell^2$), this reads
\[
\tan \xi = -\frac{\xi}{k\ell}, \qquad \xi > 0.
\]

#### Graphical and analytic proof of infinitely many eigenvalues

Plot the curves $Y_1(\xi) = \tan\xi$ and $Y_2(\xi) = -\frac{\xi}{k\ell}$ for $\xi > 0$:

```text
   Y ^
     |    |     :    /      :     /      :     /
     |    |     :   /       :    /       :    /
     |   /      :  /        :   /        :   /
     |  /       : /         :  /         :  /
  ---+--+-------+---+-------+----+-------+----+------> xi
    0|\  pi/2   :  pi      3pi/2 : 2pi  5pi/2 : 3pi
     | \        :/          :   /        :   /
     |  \      /:           :  /         :  /
     |   \____* :           : /          : /      <-- Y = tan(xi)
     |        :\            :/           :/
     |        : \__________*             /        <-- Y = -xi/(k*ell)
     |        :            :\           /
     |        :            : \_________*          (roots xi_1, xi_2, xi_3, ...)
     |     xi=pi/2      xi=3pi/2     xi=5pi/2     (vertical asymptotes)
```

Key features read directly from the graph (and verified analytically):

1. The straight line $Y_2(\xi) = -\frac{\xi}{k\ell}$ passes through the origin with negative slope $-\frac{1}{k\ell} < 0$, so $Y_2(\xi) < 0$ for all $\xi > 0$.
2. On $(0, \frac{\pi}{2})$ and on every interval $(n\pi, (n+\frac{1}{2})\pi)$ ($n \ge 1$), $\tan\xi > 0 > -\frac{\xi}{k\ell}$, so there are **no** roots there. At $\xi = n\pi$ ($n \ge 1$), $\tan(n\pi) = 0 > -\frac{n\pi}{k\ell}$, so $\xi = n\pi$ is never a root. At the vertical asymptotes $\xi = (n-\frac{1}{2})\pi$, $\cos\xi = 0$, already ruled out.
3. For every integer $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, consider the open interval
   \[
   I_n := \left(\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi,\; n\pi\right),
   \]
   and define $h(\xi) := \tan\xi + \frac{\xi}{k\ell}$ on $I_n$.
   - $h$ is continuous and differentiable on $I_n$, with
     \[
     h'(\xi) = \sec^2\xi + \frac{1}{k\ell} > 0 \qquad \forall \xi \in I_n,
     \]
     so $h$ is **strictly increasing** on $I_n$ (equivalently, $\tan\xi$ increases strictly from $-\infty$ to $0$ while $-\frac{\xi}{k\ell}$ decreases strictly).
   - $\lim_{\xi \to (n - \frac{1}{2})\pi^+} h(\xi) = -\infty$, while $h(n\pi) = \frac{n\pi}{k\ell} > 0$.

   By the Intermediate Value Theorem and strict monotonicity, $h(\xi) = 0$ has a **unique** root $\xi_n \in \left((n - \frac{1}{2})\pi,\; n\pi\right)$ for every integer $n \ge 1$.

Since the intervals $I_n$ are pairwise disjoint and strictly ordered, there exist infinitely many positive roots
\[
0 < \xi_1 < \xi_2 < \cdots < \xi_n < \cdots, \qquad \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi < \xi_n < n\pi,
\]
and hence infinitely many strictly increasing positive eigenvalues
\[
0 < \lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \cdots < \lambda_n < \cdots, \qquad \lambda_n = \mu_n^2 = \frac{\xi_n^2}{\ell^2},
\]
with corresponding eigenfunctions
\[
y_n(x) = C_n \sin(\mu_n x) = C_n \sin\!\left(\frac{\xi_n x}{\ell}\right) \qquad (C_n \neq 0).
\]

#### Behaviour of $\lambda_n$ as $n \to \infty$

From the graph (or equation $\tan\xi_n = -\frac{\xi_n}{k\ell}$), as $n \to \infty$ we have $\xi_n > (n-\frac{1}{2})\pi \to \infty$, so $-\frac{\xi_n}{k\ell} \to -\infty$. On the interval $I_n = ((n-\frac{1}{2})\pi, n\pi)$, $\tan\xi_n \to -\infty$ forces $\xi_n$ to approach the **left** endpoint (the vertical asymptote) $\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi$ from above.

To obtain the sharp asymptotic expansion, write
\[
\xi_n = \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi + \delta_n, \qquad 0 < \delta_n < \frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
Using $\tan\!\left((n - \frac{1}{2})\pi + \delta_n\right) = -\cot\delta_n$, the eigenvalue equation $\tan\xi_n = -\frac{\xi_n}{k\ell}$ becomes
\[
\cot\delta_n = \frac{\xi_n}{k\ell}, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \tan\delta_n = \frac{k\ell}{\xi_n}.
\]
Since $\xi_n = (n-\frac{1}{2})\pi + O(1) \to \infty$, we have $\tan\delta_n \to 0^+$, so $\delta_n \to 0^+$. Let $X_n := \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi$. Then
\[
\delta_n = \arctan\!\left(\frac{k\ell}{X_n + \delta_n}\right) = \frac{k\ell}{X_n + \delta_n} - \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{k\ell}{X_n}\right)^3 + O\!\left(\frac{1}{X_n^5}\right) = \frac{k\ell}{X_n} - \frac{(k\ell)^2 + \frac{1}{3}(k\ell)^3}{X_n^3} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{X_n^5}\right).
\]
Hence
\[
\mu_n = \frac{\xi_n}{\ell} = \frac{\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi}{\ell} + \frac{k}{\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^3}\right),
\]
and squaring gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_n = \frac{\xi_n^2}{\ell^2} = \frac{(X_n + \delta_n)^2}{\ell^2} &= \frac{1}{\ell^2}\!\left(X_n^2 + 2X_n\delta_n + \delta_n^2\right) \\
&= \frac{1}{\ell^2}\!\left(X_n^2 + 2k\ell - \frac{(k\ell)^2 + \frac{2}{3}(k\ell)^3}{X_n^2} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{X_n^4}\right)\right) \\
&= \frac{\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)^2\pi^2}{\ell^2} + \frac{2k}{\ell} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right) \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty.
\end{aligned}
\]
In particular:
- $\lambda_n \to \infty$ quadratically in $n$, with leading asymptotic behaviour $\lambda_n \sim \dfrac{n^2 \pi^2}{\ell^2}$ (more precisely, $\lambda_n = \dfrac{(n-\frac{1}{2})^2\pi^2}{\ell^2} + \dfrac{2k}{\ell} + O(n^{-2})$).
- Intuitively, for high modes the derivative term $y'(\ell) \sim \mu_n y$ dominates $k y(\ell)$ in the Robin condition $k y(\ell) + y'(\ell) = 0$, so the spectrum approaches that of the Dirichlet–Neumann problem ($y(0)=0, y'(\ell)=0$, with eigenvalues $(n-\frac{1}{2})^2\pi^2/\ell^2$) from above, shifted asymptotically by the constant $+\frac{2k}{\ell}$.

## Final Answers

- **Self-adjointness:** $\langle u, \mathcal{L}v\rangle - \langle\mathcal{L}u, v\rangle = [p((u^*)'v - u^*v')]_a^b = 0$ by the separated real boundary conditions with $(A_1,A_2)\neq(0,0)$ and $(B_1,B_2)\neq(0,0)$.
- **Rayleigh quotient:** $\displaystyle\lambda = \frac{\int_a^b(p{y'}^2 + qy^2)\,dx - [pyy']_a^b}{\int_a^b \omega y^2\,dx}$.
- **Strict positivity ($p=\omega=1, q\ge 0, y(0)=0, ky(\ell)+y'(\ell)=0, k>0$):**
  $\displaystyle\lambda = \frac{\int_0^\ell({y'}^2 + qy^2)\,dx + ky(\ell)^2}{\int_0^\ell y^2\,dx} > 0$ (since $\lambda = 0 \implies y' \equiv 0$ and $y(0) = 0 \implies y \equiv 0$, contradiction).
- **Explicit solution ($q = 0$):**
  \[
  y_n(x) = C_n \sin(\mu_n x) \quad (C_n \neq 0), \qquad \lambda_n = \mu_n^2,
  \]
  where $0 < \mu_1 < \mu_2 < \cdots$ are the infinitely many positive roots of
  \[
  \tan(\mu\ell) = -\frac{\mu}{k}, \qquad \mu_n \in \left(\frac{\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi}{\ell},\; \frac{n\pi}{\ell}\right) \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots).
  \]
- **Asymptotic behaviour as $n \to \infty$:**
  \[
  \lambda_n = \frac{\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)^2\pi^2}{\ell^2} + \frac{2k}{\ell} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right) \sim \frac{n^2\pi^2}{\ell^2} \qquad (n \to \infty).
  \]
