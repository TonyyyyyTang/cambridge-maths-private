## Full Solution

### 1. Method of Lagrange multipliers

Let $f, g : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuously differentiable ($C^1$) functions, and suppose we seek the stationary points of $f(x, y)$ subject to the constraint $g(x, y) = 0$.

1. **Form the Lagrangian:** Introduce an auxiliary scalar parameter $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ (the *Lagrange multiplier*) and define
   $$
   \mathcal{L}(x, y, \lambda) = f(x, y) - \lambda g(x, y)
   $$
   (equivalently $f(x, y) + \lambda g(x, y)$).
2. **Solve the unconstrained stationarity equations:** Require $\mathcal{L}$ to be stationary with respect to all three variables $(x, y, \lambda)$:
   $$
   \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} - \lambda \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad
   \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} - \lambda \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 0, \qquad
   \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = -g(x, y) = 0.
   $$
   Equivalently, we solve the system of three equations in three unknowns $(x, y, \lambda)$:
   $$
   \nabla f(x, y) = \lambda \nabla g(x, y), \qquad g(x, y) = 0.
   $$
3. **Justification & regularity hypothesis:** Along any smooth curve $\mathbf{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t))$ lying in the constraint locus $g(x, y) = 0$ and passing through $(a, b) = \mathbf{r}(0)$ with nonzero tangent $\mathbf{r}'(0)$, differentiating $g(\mathbf{r}(t)) = 0$ gives $\nabla g(a, b) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(0) = 0$. Thus, provided $\nabla g(a, b) \neq \mathbf{0}$ (the regularity condition), $\nabla g(a, b)$ is normal to the constraint curve at $(a, b)$. For $(a, b)$ to be a stationary point of $f$ along the curve, we require $\left.\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf{r}(t))\right|_{t=0} = \nabla f(a, b)\cdot\mathbf{r}'(0) = 0$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, any vector orthogonal to the nonzero tangent $\mathbf{r}'(0)$ must be parallel to the nonzero normal $\nabla g(a, b)$, so there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\nabla f(a, b) = \lambda \nabla g(a, b)$.
   *(One must also check any singular points of the constraint where $g(a, b) = 0$ and $\nabla g(a, b) = \mathbf{0}$ separately, since the representation $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ requires $\nabla g \neq \mathbf{0}$.)*

### 2. Vanishing of the Jacobian determinant at a stationary point $(a, b)$

Let $(a, b)$ be a stationary point of $f$ subject to $g(x, y) = 0$.

- **Case 1 (regular point, $\nabla g(a, b) \neq \mathbf{0}$):** By the method of Lagrange multipliers, there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
  $$
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b) = \lambda \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b), \qquad
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) = \lambda \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b).
  $$
  This says that the first column of the $2\times 2$ matrix
  $$
  M = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b) & \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b) \\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) & \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) \end{pmatrix}
  $$
  is $\lambda$ times the second column (equivalently $M\binom{1}{-\lambda} = \binom{0}{0}$ has a nontrivial solution). Directly expanding the determinant confirms:
  $$
  \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b) & \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b) \\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) & \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) \end{vmatrix}
  = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b)\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) - \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b)\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b)
  = \lambda \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b)\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) - \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b)\left(\lambda \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b)\right) = 0.
  $$

- **Case 2 (singular point, $\nabla g(a, b) = \mathbf{0}$):** The second column of the determinant is $(0, 0)^T$, so the determinant vanishes trivially in this case as well.

Therefore, at every constrained stationary point $(a, b)$,
$$
\begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b) & \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b) \\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) & \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) \end{vmatrix} = 0.
$$

### 3. Maximum distance from the origin to $x^2 + y^2 + xy - 4 = 0$

The distance from the origin to $(x, y)$ is $d(x, y) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. Since $r \mapsto r^2$ is strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$ (and $(0, 0)$ does not lie on the curve since $0 + 0 + 0 - 4 = -4 \neq 0$), maximizing $d(x, y)$ is equivalent to maximizing the smooth squared-distance function
$$
f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2
$$
subject to the constraint
$$
g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 + xy - 4 = 0.
$$

**Existence of a global maximum and absence of singular points:**
For all $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
$$
x^2 + y^2 + xy = \frac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2) + \frac{1}{2}(x + y)^2 \geqslant \frac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2),
$$
so every point on $g(x, y) = 0$ satisfies $x^2 + y^2 \leqslant 8$. The constraint curve is therefore closed and bounded (hence compact — indeed, it is an ellipse). By the Extreme Value Theorem, the continuous function $f$ attains its global maximum (and minimum) on this curve. Furthermore,
$$
\nabla g(x, y) = (2x + y,\; x + 2y) = (0, 0) \iff x = y = 0,
$$
which does not lie on $g(x, y) = 0$. Hence every point on the curve is regular, and the global maximum must occur at one of the stationary points given by the determinant condition.

**Finding the stationary points:**
Compute the partial derivatives:
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y, \qquad
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 2x + y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = x + 2y.
$$
Applying the determinant condition from Part 2:
$$
0 = \begin{vmatrix} 2x & 2x + y \\ 2y & x + 2y \end{vmatrix}
= 2x(x + 2y) - 2y(2x + y)
= 2x^2 + 4xy - 4xy - 2y^2
= 2(x^2 - y^2)
= 2(x - y)(x + y).
$$
Hence either $y = x$ or $y = -x$. (Equivalently, $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ gives $2x = \lambda(2x+y)$ and $2y = \lambda(x+2y)$; subtracting gives $(2-\lambda)(x-y) = 0$ and adding gives $(2-3\lambda)(x+y) = 0$, yielding $y = x$ with $\lambda = \frac{2}{3}$ or $y = -x$ with $\lambda = 2$.)

- **Case 1: $y = x$.** Substituting into $g(x, y) = 0$:
  $$
  x^2 + x^2 + x^2 - 4 = 0 \implies 3x^2 = 4 \implies x = y = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}.
  $$
  At the two points $\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$ and $\left(-\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}, -\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$,
  $$
  f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 = \frac{8}{3}, \qquad d(x, y) = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3}.
  $$

- **Case 2: $y = -x$.** Substituting into $g(x, y) = 0$:
  $$
  x^2 + x^2 - x^2 - 4 = 0 \implies x^2 = 4 \implies (x, y) = (2, -2) \text{ or } (-2, 2).
  $$
  At these two points,
  $$
  f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 = 8, \qquad d(x, y) = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}.
  $$

Comparing the stationary values on the compact curve, since $8 > \frac{8}{3}$, the global maximum of $x^2 + y^2$ is $8$ (attained at $(2, -2)$ and $(-2, 2)$), and therefore the **maximum distance** from the origin to the curve is
$$
d_{\max} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}.
$$

*(Direct geometric check: rotating by $45^\circ$ via $u = (x+y)/\sqrt{2}$, $v = (x-y)/\sqrt{2}$ transforms the curve into $\frac{3}{2}u^2 + \frac{1}{2}v^2 = 4$, i.e. $\frac{u^2}{8/3} + \frac{v^2}{8} = 1$, an ellipse centered at the origin with semi-minor axis $\sqrt{8/3}$ and semi-major axis $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$.)*
