## Full Solution

### 1. Proof of the solvability condition $(*)$

We are given a non-zero solution $y_0(x)$ of
\[L(y_0; \lambda_0) \equiv \frac{d}{dx}\left(p(x)\frac{dy_0}{dx}\right) + \bigl(q(x) + \lambda_0 w(x)\bigr)y_0 = 0, \qquad y_0(0) = y_0(1) = 0,\]
and functions $y(x)$, $f(x)$ satisfying
\[L(y; \lambda_0) = f, \qquad y(0) = y(1) = 0.\]
(Assuming standard Sturm–Liouville regularity so that $p y_0'$, $p y'$ are continuously differentiable on $[0,1]$ and bounded at the endpoints.)

Multiply $L(y; \lambda_0) = f$ by $y_0(x)$ and integrate over $[0,1]$:
\[\int_0^1 y_0 f\,dx = \int_0^1 y_0 L(y; \lambda_0)\,dx = \int_0^1 y_0 \frac{d}{dx}\left(p\frac{dy}{dx}\right)dx + \int_0^1 (q + \lambda_0 w)y_0 y\,dx.\]
Integrating the first term by parts twice gives
\begin{align*}
\int_0^1 y_0 \frac{d}{dx}\left(p\frac{dy}{dx}\right)dx
&= \left[y_0 p \frac{dy}{dx}\right]_0^1 - \int_0^1 p \frac{dy_0}{dx}\frac{dy}{dx}\,dx \\
&= \left[p\left(y_0 \frac{dy}{dx} - y \frac{dy_0}{dx}\right)\right]_0^1 + \int_0^1 y \frac{d}{dx}\left(p\frac{dy_0}{dx}\right)dx.
\end{align*}
(Equivalently, this follows immediately from Lagrange's identity
\[y_0 L(y; \lambda_0) - y L(y_0; \lambda_0) = \frac{d}{dx}\left[p(x)\bigl(y_0(x)y'(x) - y(x)y_0'(x)\bigr)\right].\])

Therefore,
\[\int_0^1 y_0 f\,dx = \left[p(x)\bigl(y_0(x)y'(x) - y(x)y_0'(x)\bigr)\right]_0^1 + \int_0^1 y L(y_0; \lambda_0)\,dx.\]
Since $y_0(0) = y_0(1) = 0$ and $y(0) = y(1) = 0$, the boundary term vanishes at both $x = 0$ and $x = 1$. Since $L(y_0; \lambda_0) = 0$ identically on $[0,1]$, the remaining integral also vanishes. Hence
\[\int_0^1 y_0 f\,dx = 0, \qquad (*)\]
as required.

### 2. Application to the nonlinear problem $L(y; \lambda) = y^3$

Consider the nonlinear boundary value problem
\[L(y; \lambda) = y^3, \qquad y(0) = y(1) = 0,\]
where $y_0$ is normalised by
\[\int_0^1 w(x) y_0(x)^2\,dx = 1.\]

By the definition of $L$,
\[L(y; \lambda) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(p\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + \bigl(q + \lambda w\bigr)y = L(y; \lambda_0) + (\lambda - \lambda_0)w(x)y.\]
Thus the equation $L(y; \lambda) = y^3$ can be rewritten in the form $L(y; \lambda_0) = f$ by choosing
\[f(x) = y(x)^3 - (\lambda - \lambda_0)w(x)y(x).\]
Since $y(x)$ satisfies the same boundary conditions $y(0) = y(1) = 0$ as $y_0(x)$, the solvability condition $(*)$ applies directly and gives the exact relation
\[\int_0^1 y_0(x)\bigl[y(x)^3 - (\lambda - \lambda_0)w(x)y(x)\bigr]\,dx = 0,\]
that is,
\[(\lambda - \lambda_0)\int_0^1 w(x)y_0(x)y(x)\,dx = \int_0^1 y_0(x)y(x)^3\,dx.\]

Now substitute $\lambda - \lambda_0 = \epsilon^2 \mu$ (with $\mu = O(1)$, $\epsilon \ll 1$) and $y(x) = \epsilon y_0(x) + \epsilon^2 y_1(x)$ (with $y_1(x) = O(1)$ uniformly—or in integral norm—as $\epsilon \to 0$).

- **Left-hand side:**
  \begin{align*}
  (\lambda - \lambda_0)\int_0^1 w y_0 y\,dx
  &= \epsilon^2 \mu \int_0^1 w y_0 \bigl(\epsilon y_0 + \epsilon^2 y_1\bigr)\,dx \\
  &= \epsilon^3 \mu \int_0^1 w y_0^2\,dx + \epsilon^4 \mu \int_0^1 w y_0 y_1\,dx \\
  &= \epsilon^3 \mu + \epsilon^4 \mu \int_0^1 w y_0 y_1\,dx,
  \end{align*}
  where we used the normalisation $\int_0^1 w y_0^2\,dx = 1$.

- **Right-hand side:**
  \begin{align*}
  \int_0^1 y_0 y^3\,dx
  &= \int_0^1 y_0 \bigl(\epsilon y_0 + \epsilon^2 y_1\bigr)^3\,dx \\
  &= \epsilon^3 \int_0^1 y_0^4\,dx + 3\epsilon^4 \int_0^1 y_0^3 y_1\,dx + 3\epsilon^5 \int_0^1 y_0^2 y_1^2\,dx + \epsilon^6 \int_0^1 y_0 y_1^3\,dx.
  \end{align*}

Equating both sides and dividing through by $\epsilon^3$ (for $\epsilon \neq 0$) yields
\[\mu + \epsilon \mu \int_0^1 w y_0 y_1\,dx = \int_0^1 y_0^4\,dx + 3\epsilon \int_0^1 y_0^3 y_1\,dx + 3\epsilon^2 \int_0^1 y_0^2 y_1^2\,dx + \epsilon^3 \int_0^1 y_0 y_1^3\,dx.\]
Since $\mu = O(1)$ and $y_1 = O(1)$ as $\epsilon \to 0$, every term proportional to $\epsilon, \epsilon^2, \epsilon^3$ is $O(\epsilon)$. Rearranging therefore gives
\[\mu = \int_0^1 y_0^4\,dx + O(\epsilon),\]
as required.

*(Remark on the order of the remainder: if no normalisation fixing the $y_0$-component of $y_1$ is imposed, then shifting $\epsilon \mapsto \epsilon + c\epsilon^2$ gives $y_1 = c y_0 + O(\epsilon)$ and produces a genuine $O(\epsilon)$ shift $2c\epsilon\int_0^1 y_0^4\,dx$ in $\mu$. If instead the amplitude is normalised so that $\int_0^1 w y_0 y_1\,dx = 0$, then the equation $\epsilon^2 L(y_1; \lambda_0) = O(\epsilon^3)$ implies $y_1 = O(\epsilon)$ and the remainder improves to $O(\epsilon^2)$, reflecting the $y \mapsto -y$ symmetry of $L(y;\lambda) = y^3$.)*

- **Choice of $f$:** $f(x) = y(x)^3 - (\lambda - \lambda_0)w(x)y(x)$, giving $L(y; \lambda_0) = f$.
- **Asymptotic bifurcation formula:** $\mu = \int_0^1 y_0^4\,dx + O(\epsilon)$.
