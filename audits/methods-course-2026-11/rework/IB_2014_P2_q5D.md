## Full Solution

Let $\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ denote the $1$-periodic extension of $f(x) = x(1-x)$ defined on $[0,1)$, so that $\tilde{f}(x+1) = \tilde{f}(x)$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\tilde{f}(x) = x(1-x)$ for $x\in[0,1)$.

### (i) Fourier series of $\tilde{f}$

Since $\tilde{f}$ has period $L = 1$ (fundamental angular frequency $2\pi/L = 2\pi$), its real Fourier series has the form
\[
\tilde{f}(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigl(a_n \cos(2\pi n x) + b_n \sin(2\pi n x)\bigr),
\]
where
\[
a_n = 2\int_0^1 f(x)\cos(2\pi n x)\,dx \quad (n \ge 0), \qquad b_n = 2\int_0^1 f(x)\sin(2\pi n x)\,dx \quad (n \ge 1).
\]

**Evenness ($b_n = 0$):** For $x\in(0,1)$, we have $\tilde{f}(-x) = \tilde{f}(1-x) = (1-x)\bigl(1-(1-x)\bigr) = x(1-x) = \tilde{f}(x)$, and $\tilde{f}(0) = 0$. Thus $\tilde{f}$ is an even $1$-periodic function on $\mathbb{R}$, so $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$. Equivalently, substituting $u = 1-x$ gives
\[
b_n = 2\int_0^1 x(1-x)\sin(2\pi n x)\,dx = 2\int_0^1 (1-u)u\sin\bigl(2\pi n(1-u)\bigr)\,du = -2\int_0^1 u(1-u)\sin(2\pi n u)\,du = -b_n,
\]
whence $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$.

**Constant term ($n = 0$):**
\[
a_0 = 2\int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,dx = 2\left[\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = 2\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{3},
\]
so $\frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Cosine coefficients ($n \ge 1$):** Integrating by parts twice,
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_n &= 2\int_0^1 x(1-x)\cos(2\pi n x)\,dx \\
&= 2\left[x(1-x)\frac{\sin(2\pi n x)}{2\pi n}\right]_0^1 - \frac{1}{\pi n}\int_0^1 (1-2x)\sin(2\pi n x)\,dx \\
&= -\frac{1}{\pi n}\left(\left[-(1-2x)\frac{\cos(2\pi n x)}{2\pi n}\right]_0^1 - \frac{1}{\pi n}\int_0^1 \cos(2\pi n x)\,dx\right) \\
&= -\frac{1}{\pi n}\left(\left[\frac{2x-1}{2\pi n}\cos(2\pi n x)\right]_0^1 - \frac{1}{\pi n}\left[\frac{\sin(2\pi n x)}{2\pi n}\right]_0^1\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
At $x = 1$ we have $\frac{2(1)-1}{2\pi n}\cos(2\pi n) = \frac{1}{2\pi n}$, and at $x = 0$ we have $\frac{2(0)-1}{2\pi n}\cos(0) = -\frac{1}{2\pi n}$, while the sine boundary term vanishes. Hence
\[
a_n = -\frac{1}{\pi n}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi n} - \left(-\frac{1}{2\pi n}\right)\right) = -\frac{1}{\pi^2 n^2} \qquad (n \ge 1).
\]

Therefore, the Fourier series for the periodic extension of $f(x)$ is
\[
\frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(2\pi n x)}{n^2}
\]
(or equivalently in complex form $\frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}} \frac{e^{2\pi i n x}}{n^2}$). Note that since $\tilde{f}$ is continuous and piecewise smooth on $\mathbb{R}$ (and $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{\pi^2 n^2} < \infty$), this series converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ to $\tilde{f}(x)$.

### (ii) Justification of term-by-term differentiation of the Fourier series

Notice first that one **cannot** justify term-by-term differentiation on all of $\mathbb{R}$ by appealing to uniform convergence of the differentiated series, because the periodic extension $\tilde{f}'$ of $f'(x) = 1 - 2x$ has jump discontinuities at every integer $x \in \mathbb{Z}$, so its Fourier series cannot converge uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$. Instead, term-by-term differentiation yields the **Fourier series** of $\tilde{f}'$ because of the following two regularity properties of $\tilde{f}$:

1. $\tilde{f}$ is **continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$** — crucially, there is no jump discontinuity at the period endpoints $x \in \mathbb{Z}$, since
   \[
   \lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x) = f(0) = 0 = \lim_{x\to 1^-} f(x), \qquad \text{i.e. } \tilde{f}(0^+) = \tilde{f}(1^-).
   \]
2. $f$ is continuously differentiable on $(0,1)$ with $f'(x) = 1 - 2x$ bounded and smooth on $(0,1)$ (so $\tilde{f}'$ is piecewise smooth and integrable on $[0,1]$).

Let the Fourier series of the $1$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}'$ be
\[
G(x) = \frac{\alpha_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigl(\alpha_n \cos(2\pi n x) + \beta_n \sin(2\pi n x)\bigr).
\]
Computing its coefficients directly using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and integration by parts on $[0,1]$ (the isolated integer points where $\tilde{f}$ is not differentiable have measure zero and do not affect the integrals):
\[
\alpha_0 = 2\int_0^1 f'(x)\,dx = 2\bigl[f(x)\bigr]_{0^+}^{1^-} = 2\bigl(f(1^-) - f(0^+)\bigr) = 0.
\]
For $n \ge 1$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_n &= 2\int_0^1 f'(x)\cos(2\pi n x)\,dx \\
&= 2\bigl[f(x)\cos(2\pi n x)\bigr]_{0^+}^{1^-} + 4\pi n\int_0^1 f(x)\sin(2\pi n x)\,dx \\
&= 2\bigl(f(1^-) - f(0^+)\bigr) + 2\pi n b_n = 2\pi n b_n,
\end{aligned}
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
\beta_n &= 2\int_0^1 f'(x)\sin(2\pi n x)\,dx \\
&= 2\bigl[f(x)\sin(2\pi n x)\bigr]_{0^+}^{1^-} - 4\pi n\int_0^1 f(x)\cos(2\pi n x)\,dx \\
&= 0 - 2\pi n a_n = -2\pi n a_n,
\end{aligned}
\]
where the boundary terms vanish specifically because $f(1^-) = f(0^+) = 0$ (continuity of the periodic extension $\tilde{f}$ across $x \in \mathbb{Z}$, together with $\cos(2\pi n) = \cos(0) = 1$ and $\sin(2\pi n) = \sin(0) = 0$).

Comparing with the term-by-term derivative of the Fourier series of $\tilde{f}$,
\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_n\cos(2\pi n x) + b_n\sin(2\pi n x)\bigr)\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl((2\pi n b_n)\cos(2\pi n x) + (-2\pi n a_n)\sin(2\pi n x)\bigr),
\]
we see that every Fourier coefficient of $\tilde{f}'$ ($\alpha_0 = 0$, $\alpha_n = 2\pi n b_n$, $\beta_n = -2\pi n a_n$) coincides exactly with the corresponding coefficient obtained by differentiating the Fourier series of $\tilde{f}$ term by term. (In contrast, if $\tilde{f}$ had a jump discontinuity at integers so that $f(1^-) \neq f(0^+)$, the boundary terms $2(f(1^-)-f(0^+))$ in $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_n$ would be non-zero and term-by-term differentiation would fail to produce the Fourier series of $\tilde{f}'$.)

Explicitly, substituting $a_n = -\frac{1}{\pi^2 n^2}$ and $b_n = 0$ gives $\alpha_0 = 0$, $\alpha_n = 0$, and $\beta_n = -2\pi n\left(-\frac{1}{\pi^2 n^2}\right) = \frac{2}{\pi n}$, so
\[
G(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2\pi n x)}{n}.
\]
Because $\tilde{f}'$ is piecewise smooth, Dirichlet's theorem guarantees that $G(x)$ converges everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ (and equals $f'(x) = 1-2x$ for all $x \in (0,1)$).

### (iii) Value of $G(0)$ and relation to $f'$

Evaluating $G(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2\pi n x)}{n}$ at $x = 0$, every term vanishes because $\sin(0) = 0$, so
\[
G(0) = 0.
\]

**Relation to the values of $f'$:** On $(0,1)$, $f'(x) = 1 - 2x$, so its $1$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}'$ has a jump discontinuity at $x = 0$ (and at every $x \in \mathbb{Z}$), with one-sided limits
\[
\tilde{f}'(0^+) = \lim_{x\to 0^+} f'(x) = 1, \qquad \tilde{f}'(0^-) = \lim_{x\to 0^-} \tilde{f}'(x) = \lim_{x\to 1^-} f'(x) = 1 - 2(1) = -1.
\]
By Dirichlet's convergence theorem for Fourier series of piecewise smooth periodic functions, at any jump discontinuity the Fourier series converges to the arithmetic mean (midpoint) of the left-hand and right-hand limits:
\[
G(0) = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\tilde{f}'(0^+) + \tilde{f}'(0^-)\Bigr) = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(f'(0^+) + f'(1^-)\Bigr) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + (-1)\bigr) = 0.
\]

## Final Answers

- **(i)** The Fourier series for the $1$-periodic extension of $f(x) = x(1-x)$ on $[0,1)$ is
  \[
  \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(2\pi n x)}{n^2}.
  \]
- **(ii)** Because the $1$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$ (in particular $f(0^+) = f(1^-) = 0$) and piecewise smooth (with $f'(x) = 1-2x$ continuous/integrable on $(0,1)$), integration by parts of the Fourier coefficients of $\tilde{f}'$ has vanishing boundary terms and yields $\alpha_0 = 0$, $\alpha_n = 2\pi n b_n = 0$, and $\beta_n = -2\pi n a_n = \frac{2}{\pi n}$ — identical to the coefficients of the term-by-term derivative $G(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\sin(2\pi n x)}{n}$.
- **(iii)** $G(0) = 0$. This equals the average of the left-hand and right-hand limits of the periodic extension of $f'$ at $x = 0$ (Dirichlet's theorem at a jump discontinuity): $G(0) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(f'(0^+) + f'(1^-)\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + (-1)\bigr) = 0$.
