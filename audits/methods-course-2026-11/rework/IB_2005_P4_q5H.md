## Full Solution

Throughout we assume $c \neq 0$ (the wave speed, without loss of generality $c > 0$), $L > 0$, and $y \in C^2$.

### Part 1: General (d'Alembert) solution of the wave equation

Introduce the characteristic coordinates
$$
u = ct - x, \qquad v = ct + x.
$$
The Jacobian of the coordinate transformation $(x, t) \mapsto (u, v)$ is
$$
\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, t)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} -1 & c \\ 1 & c \end{pmatrix} = -2c \neq 0,
$$
so the map is an invertible smooth change of variables with inverse $x = \frac{v - u}{2}$, $t = \frac{u + v}{2c}$.

Writing $Y(u, v) = y(x(u, v), t(u, v))$, the chain rule gives
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial v} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial}{\partial v},
$$
$$
\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial}{\partial v}.
$$
Since $y \in C^2$, mixed partial derivatives commute ($\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u\partial v} = \frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial v\partial u}$), so
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} - \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}
&= \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)^2 Y - \left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)^2 Y \\
&= \left(\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u^2} + 2\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u\partial v} + \frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial v^2}\right) - \left(\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u^2} - 2\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u\partial v} + \frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial v^2}\right) \\
&= 4\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u\partial v}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the wave equation is equivalent to
$$
\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u\partial v} = 0.
$$
Integrating first with respect to $v$ (at fixed $u$) shows that $\frac{\partial Y}{\partial u}$ is independent of $v$, i.e.
$$
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial u} = h(u)
$$
for some arbitrary $C^1$ function $h$ of $u$ alone. Integrating next with respect to $u$ (at fixed $v$) yields
$$
Y(u, v) = f(u) + g(v),
$$
where $f(u) = \int h(u)\,\mathrm{d}u$ is an arbitrary $C^2$ function of $u$ and $g(v)$ is an arbitrary $C^2$ function of $v$ (the "constant" of integration in $u$). Substituting back $u = ct - x$ and $v = ct + x$ gives the general solution
$$
y(x, t) = f(ct - x) + g(ct + x).
$$
(Conversely, direct differentiation shows that any such $y$ with $f, g \in C^2$ satisfies $\frac{1}{c^2}y_{tt} - y_{xx} = (f''(ct-x) + g''(ct+x)) - (f''(ct-x) + g''(ct+x)) = 0$.)

### Part 2: Boundary conditions $y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0$

Imposing $y(0, t) = 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$ gives
$$
f(ct) + g(ct) = 0 \qquad \forall t \in \mathbb{R}.
$$
Setting $s = ct$ (which ranges over all of $\mathbb{R}$ since $c \neq 0$), we obtain the relation
$$
f(s) = -g(s) \qquad \forall s \in \mathbb{R},
$$
so that $y(x, t) = g(ct + x) - g(ct - x)$.

Imposing $y(L, t) = 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$ gives
$$
f(ct - L) + g(ct + L) = 0 \qquad \forall t \in \mathbb{R}.
$$
Using $f = -g$ and $s = ct$, this becomes
$$
-g(s - L) + g(s + L) = 0 \implies g(s + L) = g(s - L) \qquad \forall s \in \mathbb{R}.
$$
Putting $\xi = s - L$ (which again ranges over all of $\mathbb{R}$), we obtain
$$
g(\xi + 2L) = g(\xi) \qquad \forall \xi \in \mathbb{R}.
$$
Therefore $g$ is periodic with period $2L$. Since $f(\xi) = -g(\xi)$ for all $\xi \in \mathbb{R}$, we immediately have
$$
f(\xi + 2L) = -g(\xi + 2L) = -g(\xi) = f(\xi) \qquad \forall \xi \in \mathbb{R},
$$
so $f$ is also periodic with period $2L$.

### Part 3: Energy integral identity and time independence

With $y(x, t) = g(ct + x) - g(ct - x)$, we compute the first partial derivatives:
$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = c\,g'(ct + x) - c\,g'(ct - x) \implies \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = g'(ct + x) - g'(ct - x),
$$
$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = g'(ct + x) + g'(ct - x).
$$
Squaring and adding, the cross terms $\mp 2g'(ct+x)g'(ct-x)$ cancel:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{c^2}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2
&= \bigl(g'(ct + x) - g'(ct - x)\bigr)^2 + \bigl(g'(ct + x) + g'(ct - x)\bigr)^2 \\
&= 2\Bigl(g'(ct + x)^2 + g'(ct - x)^2\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Multiplying by $\frac{1}{2}$ and integrating over $x \in [0, L]$ gives
$$
\frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \left(\frac{1}{c^2}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2\right)\,\mathrm{d}x
= \int_0^L g'(ct + x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L g'(ct - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$
In the second integral, substitute $\tilde{x} = -x$ (so $\mathrm{d}x = -\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}$, and as $x$ goes from $0$ to $L$, $\tilde{x}$ goes from $0$ to $-L$):
$$
\int_0^L g'(ct - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^{-L} g'(ct + \tilde{x})^2\,(-\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}) = \int_{-L}^0 g'(ct + \tilde{x})^2\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}.
$$
Relabelling the dummy variable $\tilde{x}$ as $x$ and combining the integrals over $[-L, 0]$ and $[0, L]$ yields
$$
E(t) := \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \left(\frac{1}{c^2}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2\right)\,\mathrm{d}x
= \int_{-L}^0 g'(ct + x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L g'(ct + x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x
= \int_{-L}^L g'(ct + x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

To show that $E(t)$ is a constant independent of $t$, make the substitution $w = ct + x$ (at fixed $t$, $\mathrm{d}w = \mathrm{d}x$):
$$
E(t) = \int_{ct - L}^{ct + L} g'(w)^2\,\mathrm{d}w.
$$
Since $g$ is differentiable and periodic with period $2L$, differentiating $g(w + 2L) = g(w)$ gives
$$
g'(w + 2L) = g'(w) \qquad \forall w \in \mathbb{R},
$$
so $g'(w)^2$ is continuous and periodic with period $2L$. Differentiating $E(t)$ with respect to $t$ by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = c\,g'(ct + L)^2 - c\,g'(ct - L)^2 = c\Bigl(g'\bigl((ct - L) + 2L\bigr)^2 - g'(ct - L)^2\Bigr) = 0
$$
for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Hence $E(t)$ is constant, independent of $t$ (specifically $E(t) = E(0) = \int_{-L}^L g'(w)^2\,\mathrm{d}w$).

*(Alternatively, without differentiating $E(t)$: writing $E(t) - E(0) = \int_L^{ct+L} g'(w)^2\,\mathrm{d}w - \int_{-L}^{ct-L} g'(w)^2\,\mathrm{d}w$ and substituting $w = \tilde{w} + 2L$ in the first integral shows $E(t) = E(0)$ directly from $2L$-periodicity of $g'$.)*

## Final Answers

- Characteristic coordinates $u = ct - x$, $v = ct + x$ reduce the wave equation to $4\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial u\partial v} = 0$, giving $y(x, t) = f(ct - x) + g(ct + x)$.
- The boundary conditions $y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0$ require $f(s) = -g(s)$ for all $s$ and $g(\xi + 2L) = g(\xi)$, $f(\xi + 2L) = f(\xi)$ for all $\xi$ (period $2L$).
- With these boundary conditions, the energy integral satisfies
  $$
  E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \left(\frac{1}{c^2}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2\right)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{-L}^L g'(ct + x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{-L}^L g'(w)^2\,\mathrm{d}w = \text{const},
  $$
  independent of $t$.
