### Fourier Transform Convention

We use the standard Cambridge Part IB Methods convention:
\[
\tilde{f}(k) = \mathcal{F}\{f\}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx,
\qquad
f(x) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\tilde{f}\}(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tilde{f}(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk.
\]
(Note that evaluating $\tilde{f}(0) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-a^2 x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}/a$ confirms that this non-unitary normalisation is the one required by part (a).)

### Part (a)

Let $f(x) = e^{-a^2 x^2}$ with $a > 0$. We state and justify the two properties of the Fourier transform that we use:

1. **Derivative in physical space**: If $f$ is differentiable, $f, f' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, and $f(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$, then integrating by parts yields
   \[
   \widetilde{f'}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f'(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx = \Big[f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + ik\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx = ik\,\tilde{f}(k).
   \]
2. **Differentiation in $k$-space (multiplication by $x$)**: If $f(x)$ and $x f(x)$ are in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, then differentiation under the integral sign (justified by dominated convergence since $\big|\frac{\partial}{\partial k}(f(x)e^{-ikx})\big| = |x f(x)| \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$) yields
   \[
   \frac{d\tilde{f}}{dk}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (-ix)\,f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx = -i\,\widetilde{xf}(k),
   \qquad\text{so}\qquad
   \widetilde{xf}(k) = i\frac{d\tilde{f}}{dk}(k).
   \]

For $f(x) = e^{-a^2 x^2}$ ($a > 0$), both $f(x)$ and $x f(x)$ decay faster than any inverse power of $|x|$ as $|x| \to \infty$, so both properties hold. Moreover, $f$ satisfies the first-order linear ODE
\[
f'(x) = -2a^2 x f(x).
\]
Taking the Fourier transform of both sides and using linearity together with Properties 1 and 2 gives
\[
ik\,\tilde{f}(k) = -2a^2 \left(i\frac{d\tilde{f}}{dk}\right)
\implies
\frac{d\tilde{f}}{dk} = -\frac{k}{2a^2}\,\tilde{f}(k).
\]
Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{k^2/(4a^2)}$ gives
\[
\frac{d}{dk}\!\left(e^{\frac{k^2}{4a^2}}\tilde{f}(k)\right) = 0
\implies
\tilde{f}(k) = C\,e^{-\frac{k^2}{4a^2}}
\]
for a constant $C = \tilde{f}(0)$. Evaluating at $k = 0$ via the substitution $t = ax$ ($a > 0$, so $dx = dt/a$ and the integration limits remain $-\infty$ to $\infty$):
\[
C = \tilde{f}(0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-a^2 x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2}\,dt = \frac{2}{a}\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-t^2}\,dt = \frac{2}{a}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{a},
\]
using the evenness of $e^{-t^2}$ and the given hint $\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2}\,dt = \sqrt{\pi}/2$. Hence
\[
\tilde{f}(k) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{a}\,e^{-\frac{k^2}{4a^2}}.
\]

### Part (b)

We consider the Cauchy problem for the 1D diffusion equation with $D > 0$:
\[
\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2 \theta}{\partial x^2} \quad (x \in \mathbb{R},\; t > 0),
\qquad
\theta(x,0) = g(x).
\]

#### Derivation of the solution $\theta(x,t)$

Assuming $\theta(\cdot, t)$ and $\theta_x(\cdot, t)$ decay to $0$ as $|x| \to \infty$, take the spatial Fourier transform $\tilde{\theta}(k,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \theta(x,t)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx$. Applying the derivative property twice gives $\widetilde{\partial_x^2 \theta}(k,t) = (ik)^2 \tilde{\theta}(k,t) = -k^2\tilde{\theta}(k,t)$, so the PDE transforms into an ODE in $t$ for each fixed $k$:
\[
\frac{\partial \tilde{\theta}}{\partial t}(k,t) = -D k^2 \tilde{\theta}(k,t), \qquad \tilde{\theta}(k,0) = \tilde{g}(k).
\]
Solving this first-order ODE gives
\[
\tilde{\theta}(k,t) = \tilde{g}(k)\,e^{-D k^2 t}.
\]

We invert this product using the **convolution theorem**. Defining $(g * K)(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(y) K(x-y)\,dy$, we have (substituting $u = x - y$):
\[
\widetilde{g * K}(k)
= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-ikx}\!\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(y)\,K(x-y)\,dy\right)dx
= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(y)\,e^{-iky}\!\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K(u)\,e^{-iku}\,du\right)dy
= \tilde{g}(k)\,\tilde{K}(k).
\]
We now identify the heat kernel $K(x,t)$ whose spatial Fourier transform is $\tilde{K}(k,t) = e^{-Dk^2 t}$ for $t > 0$. From part (a), for any $a > 0$,
\[
\mathcal{F}\!\left\{\frac{a}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-a^2 x^2}\right\}\!(k) = e^{-\frac{k^2}{4a^2}}.
\]
Setting $\frac{1}{4a^2} = Dt$, i.e. $a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4Dt}} > 0$ (since $D > 0$ and $t > 0$), gives
\[
K(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right),
\qquad
\tilde{K}(k,t) = e^{-D k^2 t}.
\]
(Equivalently, direct Fourier inversion $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-Dk^2 t}e^{ikx}\,dk$ applies part (a) with $k$ as the integration variable and $a = \sqrt{Dt}$, giving $\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{Dt}}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)} = K(x,t)$.)

Hence, by the convolution theorem, the solution for $t > 0$ is
\[
\theta(x,t) = (g * K(\cdot,t))(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(y)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt}\right)dy.
\]

#### Checking Property P for the diffusion equation

**Yes, Property P holds for the diffusion equation.**

Suppose $g(x)$ vanishes for $|x| > \alpha$ (where $\alpha > 0$), is non-negative everywhere, and is positive on a bounded region (specifically, $g$ is non-negative and strictly positive on some non-empty subinterval—or set of positive measure—within $[-\alpha,\alpha]$, so that $\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha} g(y)\,dy > 0$). Then for any $\epsilon > 0$ (however small) and any $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ (however large),
\[
\theta(\beta, \epsilon) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D \epsilon}} \int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha} g(y)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\beta - y)^2}{4D\epsilon}\right)dy.
\]
For every $y \in [-\alpha, \alpha]$, we have $|\beta - y| \le |\beta| + \alpha$, and hence the heat kernel obeys the strictly positive lower bound
\[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D \epsilon}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\beta - y)^2}{4D\epsilon}\right)
\ge
\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D \epsilon}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(|\beta|+\alpha)^2}{4D\epsilon}\right) > 0.
\]
Since $g(y) \ge 0$ everywhere on $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ and $\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha} g(y)\,dy > 0$, it follows that
\[
\theta(\beta, \epsilon) \ge \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D \epsilon}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(|\beta|+\alpha)^2}{4D\epsilon}\right) \int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha} g(y)\,dy > 0.
\]
Thus $\theta(\beta, \epsilon)$ is strictly positive (in particular, non-zero) for **every** $\epsilon > 0$ and **every** $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$. The diffusion equation therefore exhibits infinite speed of propagation, and Property P holds.

### Part (c)

We consider the Cauchy problem for the 1D wave equation:
\[
\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},
\qquad
u(x,0) = \phi(x),
\qquad
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,0) = 0,
\]
where $c$ is a real constant.

#### Derivation of the solution $u(x,t)$ (d'Alembert's formula)

- **Case $c \neq 0$**: Introduce characteristic coordinates $\xi = x - ct$ and $\eta = x + ct$. By the chain rule,
  \[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \xi} + \frac{\partial}{\partial \eta},
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t} = -c\frac{\partial}{\partial \xi} + c\frac{\partial}{\partial \eta}.
  \]
  Hence the wave operator factorises as
  \[
  \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - c^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}
  = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} - c\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\!\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + c\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)u
  = \left(-2c\frac{\partial}{\partial \xi}\right)\!\left(2c\frac{\partial}{\partial \eta}\right)u
  = -4c^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial \xi\,\partial \eta} = 0.
  \]
  Since $c \neq 0$, $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial \xi\,\partial \eta} = 0$. Integrating with respect to $\eta$ gives $\frac{\partial u}{\partial \xi} = h(\xi)$, and integrating with respect to $\xi$ gives the general solution
  \[
  u(x,t) = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)
  \]
  for arbitrary twice-differentiable functions $F, G$. Imposing the initial conditions at $t = 0$:
  \[
  u(x,0) = F(x) + G(x) = \phi(x),
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,0) = -cF'(x) + cG'(x) = 0.
  \]
  Since $c \neq 0$, the second condition gives $G'(x) = F'(x)$, so $G(x) = F(x) + K$ for a constant $K$. Substituting into the first condition gives $2F(x) + K = \phi(x)$, hence $F(s) = \frac{1}{2}\phi(s) - \frac{K}{2}$ and $G(s) = \frac{1}{2}\phi(s) + \frac{K}{2}$. The constant $K$ cancels in the sum, yielding d'Alembert's formula:
  \[
  u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\big(\phi(x - ct) + \phi(x + ct)\big).
  \]
- **Case $c = 0$**: The PDE reduces to $\partial^2 u/\partial t^2 = 0$; integrating twice with $u(x,0) = \phi(x)$ and $u_t(x,0) = 0$ gives $u(x,t) = \phi(x)$, which is also given by $\frac{1}{2}(\phi(x - ct) + \phi(x + ct))$.

(Remark: Taking the spatial Fourier transform gives $\tilde{u}_{tt} = -c^2 k^2 \tilde{u}$ with $\tilde{u}(k,0) = \tilde{\phi}(k)$, $\tilde{u}_t(k,0) = 0$, so $\tilde{u}(k,t) = \tilde{\phi}(k)\cos(ckt) = \frac{1}{2}\tilde{\phi}(k)(e^{ickt}+e^{-ickt})$, whose inverse transform immediately recovers $u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}(\phi(x+ct) + \phi(x-ct))$ for all $c \in \mathbb{R}$.)

#### Checking Property P for the wave equation

**No, Property P does NOT hold for the wave equation.**

Suppose $\phi(x) = 0$ for all $|x| > \alpha$ (with $\alpha \ge 0$). At $(x,t) = (\beta, \epsilon)$ with $\epsilon > 0$,
\[
u(\beta, \epsilon) = \frac{1}{2}\big(\phi(\beta - c\epsilon) + \phi(\beta + c\epsilon)\big).
\]
By the reverse triangle inequality,
\[
|\beta \pm c\epsilon| \ge |\beta| - |c|\epsilon.
\]
Consequently, whenever
\[
|\beta| > \alpha + |c|\epsilon
\]
(in particular, for any fixed $\epsilon > 0$ and all sufficiently large $|\beta|$, or for any fixed $|\beta| > \alpha$ and all sufficiently small $\epsilon > 0$, namely $0 < \epsilon < (|\beta| - \alpha)/|c|$ if $c \neq 0$, or any $\epsilon > 0$ if $c = 0$), we have **both** $|\beta - c\epsilon| > \alpha$ and $|\beta + c\epsilon| > \alpha$. Therefore $\phi(\beta - c\epsilon) = \phi(\beta + c\epsilon) = 0$, forcing
\[
u(\beta, \epsilon) = 0
\]
for **every** choice of initial data $\phi$ vanishing outside $[-\alpha, \alpha]$. Hence the solution cannot become non-zero arbitrarily far away after an arbitrarily short time: disturbances propagate at a finite speed $|c|$, with support at time $\epsilon$ contained in $[-\alpha - |c|\epsilon, \alpha + |c|\epsilon]$. Property P therefore fails.

## Final Answers

- **(a)** Using $\tilde{f}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx$ together with $\widetilde{f'}(k) = ik\tilde{f}(k)$ and $\widetilde{xf}(k) = i\tilde{f}'(k)$ on $f' = -2a^2 x f$, we obtain $\tilde{f}(k) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{a}e^{-k^2/(4a^2)}$ for $a > 0$.
- **(b)** **Yes, Property P holds** for the 1D diffusion equation, justified via the derived heat-kernel solution
  \[
  \theta(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(y)\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt}\right)dy,
  \]
  which is strictly positive at every $(\beta, \epsilon)$ with $\epsilon > 0$ whenever $g \ge 0$ is non-zero on $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ (with $\int_{-\alpha}^\alpha g(y)\,dy > 0$).
- **(c)** **No, Property P does not hold** for the 1D wave equation, justified via d'Alembert's solution
  \[
  u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\big(\phi(x - ct) + \phi(x + ct)\big),
  \]
  which vanishes identically whenever $|\beta| > \alpha + |c|\epsilon$ (finite propagation speed $|c|$).
