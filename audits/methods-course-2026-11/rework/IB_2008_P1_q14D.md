## Full Solution

### 1. Euler–Lagrange equation and first integral when $f = f(x, y')$

For the functional
\[I[y] = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x, y, y')\,dx\]
with fixed boundary values $y(x_1) = y_1$ and $y(x_2) = y_2$, the Euler–Lagrange equation is
\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'}\right) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 0.\]

When $f$ is independent of $y$, i.e. $f = f(x, y')$, we have $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 0$, so the Euler–Lagrange equation simplifies to
\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'}\right) = 0.\]
Integrating once gives the first integral
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'} = C,\]
where $C$ is a constant.

### 2. Light ray moving with speed $c(x)$

Along a path $y = y(x)$ from $(x_1, y_1)$ to $(x_2, y_2)$ with $x_2 > x_1$, the element of arc length is $ds = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$. Since the speed of the light ray is $c(x) > 0$, the total travel time is
\[T[y] = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{ds}{c(x)} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}{c(x)}\,dx.\]
The integrand
\[f(x, y') = \frac{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}{c(x)}\]
is independent of $y$. Applying the first integral from Part 1 gives
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'} = \frac{y'}{c(x)\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = C\]
for some constant $C$.

**Determination of the sign of $C$ and $y'$:**
Since $c(x) > 0$ and $\sqrt{1 + y'^2} > 0$, the derivative $y'(x)$ has the same sign as $C$ for every $x \in (x_1, x_2)$.
- If $C = 0$, then $y' \equiv 0$, so $y_2 = y_1$, contradicting $y_2 > y_1$.
- If $C < 0$, then $y'(x) < 0$ on $(x_1, x_2)$, so $y_2 < y_1$ (since $x_2 > x_1$), again contradicting $y_2 > y_1$.

Hence $C > 0$, and therefore $y'(x) > 0$ on $(x_1, x_2)$. Writing $C = \frac{1}{k}$ with $k > 0$, we have
\[\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = \frac{c(x)}{k}.\]
Notice that $\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} \in (0, 1)$, so $k > c(x)$ on $(x_1, x_2)$ (with $k = c(x_2)$ permitted at an endpoint with vertical tangent). Squaring both sides yields
\[\frac{y'^2}{1 + y'^2} = \frac{c^2(x)}{k^2} \implies k^2 y'^2 = c^2(x)(1 + y'^2) \implies \bigl(k^2 - c^2(x)\bigr)y'^2 = c^2(x),\]
so
\[y'^2 = \frac{c^2(x)}{k^2 - c^2(x)}.\]
Since $y' > 0$ and $c(x) > 0$, taking the positive square root gives
\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{c(x)}{\sqrt{k^2 - c^2(x)}},\]
as required.

**Integral relation for $k, x_1, x_2, y_1, y_2$:**
Integrating $\frac{dy}{dx}$ from $x_1$ to $x_2$ and applying the boundary conditions $y(x_1) = y_1$ and $y(x_2) = y_2$ gives
\[y_2 - y_1 = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{c(x)}{\sqrt{k^2 - c^2(x)}}\,dx.\]

*(Remark on minimality: since $y' \mapsto \sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ is strictly convex and $c(x) > 0$, for any other admissible path $\tilde{y}$ with the same endpoints we have $f(x, \tilde{y}') - f(x, y') \geqslant \frac{\partial f}{\partial y'}(x, y')(\tilde{y}' - y') = \frac{1}{k}(\tilde{y}' - y')$; integrating from $x_1$ to $x_2$ yields $T[\tilde{y}] - T[y] \geqslant \frac{1}{k}[(\tilde{y} - y)]_{x_1}^{x_2} = 0$, with equality iff $\tilde{y} \equiv y$. Thus the extremal path is the unique global minimiser of $T$.)*

### 3. Special case $c(x) = ax$ and $k = ax_2$

For $c(x) = ax$ to represent a positive speed on $(x_1, x_2)$ with $x_2 > x_1$ and $k^2 - c^2(x) = a^2(x_2^2 - x^2) > 0$ on $(x_1, x_2)$, we must have $a > 0$ and $0 \leqslant x_1 < x_2$.

Substituting $c(x) = ax$ and $k = ax_2$ into the differential equation gives, for $x \in (x_1, x_2)$,
\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{ax}{\sqrt{a^2 x_2^2 - a^2 x^2}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x_2^2 - x^2}}.\]
Integrating with respect to $x$ (using the substitution $u = x_2^2 - x^2$, $du = -2x\,dx$):
\[y(x) = \int \frac{x}{\sqrt{x_2^2 - x^2}}\,dx = -\sqrt{x_2^2 - x^2} + A,\]
where $A$ is a constant of integration.

Imposing the upper boundary condition $y(x_2) = y_2$ gives
\[y_2 = -\sqrt{x_2^2 - x_2^2} + A = A \implies A = y_2.\]
(Equivalently, integrating directly from $x$ to $x_2$ yields $y_2 - y(x) = \int_x^{x_2} \frac{t}{\sqrt{x_2^2 - t^2}}\,dt = \left[-\sqrt{x_2^2 - t^2}\right]_x^{x_2} = \sqrt{x_2^2 - x^2}$.)

Hence
\[y_2 - y = \sqrt{x_2^2 - x^2}.\]
Squaring both sides gives the equation of the path:
\[(y_2 - y)^2 = x_2^2 - x^2\]
(more precisely, the lower-right circular arc $y = y_2 - \sqrt{x_2^2 - x^2}$ centred at $(0, y_2)$ of radius $x_2$, with $y \leqslant y_2$ and vertical tangent at $(x_2, y_2)$; evaluating the endpoint integral relation confirms that $k = ax_2$ corresponds to endpoints satisfying $(y_2 - y_1)^2 = x_2^2 - x_1^2$).
