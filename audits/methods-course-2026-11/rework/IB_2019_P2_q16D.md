## Full Solution

### Part 1: Self-adjoint (Sturm–Liouville) form, weight function $w(x)$, interval $(a,b)$, and orthogonality

The polynomial $y = C_n^\alpha(x)$ satisfies
\[
(1-x^2)y'' - (2\alpha+1)xy' + n(n+2\alpha)y = 0, \qquad \alpha > 0.
\]
To cast this into Sturm–Liouville self-adjoint form
\[
\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(p(x)\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + \lambda_n w(x) y = 0,
\]
with eigenvalue $\lambda_n = n(n+2\alpha)$, multiply the differential equation by a weight function $w(x)$ (strictly positive on $(a,b)$) and set $p(x) = (1-x^2)w(x)$. Matching the $y'$ coefficient in $(py')' = py'' + p'y'$ requires
\[
p'(x) = -(2\alpha+1)x w(x) = -\frac{(2\alpha+1)x}{1-x^2}\,p(x).
\]
On the interval $(-1, 1)$ where $1 - x^2 > 0$, separating variables gives
\[
\frac{p'(x)}{p(x)} = \left(\alpha + \frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{-2x}{1-x^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\left(\alpha + \frac{1}{2}\right)\ln(1-x^2)\right].
\]
Integrating (choosing the positive multiplicative constant to be $1$) yields
\[
p(x) = (1 - x^2)^{\alpha + \frac{1}{2}}, \qquad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{1-x^2} = (1 - x^2)^{\alpha - \frac{1}{2}}.
\]
One checks directly that on $(-1,1)$,
\[
\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}y'\right]
= (1-x^2)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}y'' - (2\alpha+1)x(1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}y'
= (1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\big[(1-x^2)y'' - (2\alpha+1)xy'\big],
\]
so the differential equation is equivalent on $(-1,1)$ to
\[
\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{dy}{dx}\right] + \lambda_n (1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}} y = 0, \qquad \lambda_n = n(n+2\alpha).
\]

**Choice of endpoints $a = -1$, $b = 1$ and convergence:**
- Since $\alpha > 0$, we have $\alpha + \frac{1}{2} > \frac{1}{2} > 0$, so $p(x) = (1-x^2)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}$ is real and strictly positive on $(-1,1)$ and vanishes at the singular endpoints $a = -1$ and $b = 1$:
  \[
  \lim_{x\to \pm 1^{\mp}} p(x) = 0.
  \]
- Moreover, $\alpha - \frac{1}{2} > -\frac{1}{2} > -1$. Near $x = \pm 1$, $(1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}} \sim 2^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}(1\mp x)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}$, which is integrable on $(-1,1)$ even when $0 < \alpha < \frac{1}{2}$ (where $w(x) \to +\infty$ at $x = \pm 1$). Since $C_m^\alpha(x)$ and $C_n^\alpha(x)$ are polynomials (hence bounded on $[-1,1]$), the integral $\int_{-1}^1 C_m^\alpha(x)C_n^\alpha(x)w(x)\,dx$ converges absolutely for all $\alpha > 0$.

**Proof of orthogonality for $m \neq n$:**
Let $y_m(x) = C_m^\alpha(x)$ and $y_n(x) = C_n^\alpha(x)$. They satisfy
\[
(p y_m')' + \lambda_m w y_m = 0, \qquad (p y_n')' + \lambda_n w y_n = 0.
\]
Multiplying the first equation by $y_n$, the second by $y_m$, and subtracting gives
\[
y_n (p y_m')' - y_m (p y_n')' + (\lambda_m - \lambda_n) w y_m y_n = 0,
\]
i.e., by Lagrange's identity,
\[
\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[p(x)\big(y_n(x)y_m'(x) - y_m(x)y_n'(x)\big)\right] + (\lambda_m - \lambda_n) w(x) y_m(x) y_n(x) = 0.
\]
Integrating from $-1+\epsilon$ to $1-\delta$ (with $0 < \epsilon, \delta < 1$) and letting $\epsilon, \delta \to 0^+$, we obtain
\[
\Big[(1-x^2)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\big(y_n(x)y_m'(x) - y_m(x)y_n'(x)\big)\Big]_{-1}^{1}
+ (\lambda_m - \lambda_n)\int_{-1}^{1} y_m(x) y_n(x) w(x)\,dx = 0.
\]
Because $y_m, y_n, y_m', y_n'$ are polynomials (hence bounded on $[-1,1]$) and $(1-x^2)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}} \to 0$ as $x \to \pm 1^{\mp}$ (since $\alpha > 0$), the boundary term vanishes identically. Therefore
\[
(\lambda_m - \lambda_n)\int_{-1}^{1} C_m^\alpha(x)\,C_n^\alpha(x)\,(1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,dx = 0.
\]
Now check that the eigenvalues are distinct for $m \neq n$:
\[
\lambda_m - \lambda_n = m(m+2\alpha) - n(n+2\alpha) = (m - n)(m + n + 2\alpha).
\]
Since $m, n \ge 0$ and $\alpha > 0$, we have $m + n + 2\alpha \ge 2\alpha > 0$, so $\lambda_m - \lambda_n \neq 0$ whenever $m \neq n$. Dividing by $\lambda_m - \lambda_n$ gives
\[
\int_{-1}^{1} C_m^\alpha(x)\,C_n^\alpha(x)\,(1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,dx = 0, \qquad m \neq n.
\]
Thus $a = -1$, $b = 1$, and $w(x) = (1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}$ (unique up to an arbitrary non-zero constant multiple).

### Part 2: All $n$ roots of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ lie in $(-1, 1)$

If $n = 0$, $C_0^\alpha(x)$ is a non-zero constant and has $0$ roots, so the statement holds vacuously. Assume now that $n \ge 1$.

We first establish two elementary structural properties of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ directly from its differential equation:

1. **$C_n^\alpha(x)$ is real up to a non-zero overall scalar multiple:** Writing $C_n^\alpha(x) = \sum_{j=0}^n c_j x^j$ ($c_n \neq 0$) and equating the coefficient of $x^j$ in $(1-x^2)y'' - (2\alpha+1)xy' + n(n+2\alpha)y = 0$ yields
   \[
   (j+2)(j+1)c_{j+2} + (n-j)(n+j+2\alpha)c_j = 0 \qquad (0 \le j \le n,\ c_{n+1}=c_{n+2}=0).
   \]
   Since $(n-j)(n+j+2\alpha) > 0$ for all $0 \le j \le n-1$ and $\alpha > 0$, we get $c_{n-1} = 0$ and every $c_j$ is uniquely determined downwards as a real multiple of $c_n$. Replacing $C_n^\alpha(x)$ by the real monic polynomial $\widetilde{C}_n^\alpha(x) = C_n^\alpha(x)/c_n$ does not change its roots or whether the integral vanishes, so without loss of generality $C_n^\alpha(x)$ has real coefficients.

2. **Every root of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ in $(-1, 1)$ is simple (multiplicity $1$):** Suppose $x_0 \in (-1, 1)$ were a repeated root of $y(x) = C_n^\alpha(x)$, so that $y(x_0) = 0$ and $y'(x_0) = 0$. Since $1 - x_0^2 \neq 0$, $x_0$ is an ordinary point of the ODE. Evaluating the ODE at $x_0$ gives $(1-x_0^2)y''(x_0) = 0 \implies y''(x_0) = 0$. By induction on $r \ge 2$, differentiating $(1-x^2)y'' - (2\alpha+1)xy' + n(n+2\alpha)y = 0$ $(r-2)$ times and evaluating at $x_0$ (where all derivatives of order $< r$ vanish) leaves $(1-x_0^2)y^{(r)}(x_0) = 0$, so $y^{(r)}(x_0) = 0$ for all $r \ge 0$. Since $y$ is a polynomial, Taylor's theorem gives $y \equiv 0$, contradicting $\deg C_n^\alpha = n$. Hence all roots of $C_n^\alpha$ in $(-1,1)$ are simple and distinct.

Now let $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k\}$ (with $0 \le k \le n$) be the roots of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ in $(-1, 1)$, and define the degree-$k$ polynomial
\[
P_k(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} (x - x_i)
\]
(taking the empty product $P_0(x) = 1$ if $k = 0$). Consider the integral
\[
I = \int_{-1}^{1} C_n^\alpha(x)\,P_k(x)\,w(x)\,dx.
\]

**Step A: Show $I \neq 0$.**
Since $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ are all the roots of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ in $(-1, 1)$ and each is simple, we can factor $C_n^\alpha(x)$ over $\mathbb{R}$ as
\[
C_n^\alpha(x) = Q(x)\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x - x_i) = Q(x)P_k(x),
\]
where $Q(x)$ is a real polynomial of degree $n - k$ that has **no zeros in $(-1, 1)$**. (Note: even without invoking simplicity first, listing $\{x_1, \ldots, x_k\}$ with multiplicity gives the exact same factorization $C_n^\alpha(x) = Q(x)P_k(x)$ with $Q$ non-vanishing on $(-1,1)$.)

Because $Q$ is continuous and never zero on the connected interval $(-1, 1)$, by the Intermediate Value Theorem $Q(x)$ has strictly constant sign on $(-1, 1)$: either $Q(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (-1,1)$ or $Q(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (-1,1)$.

Now write the integrand of $I$ as
\[
C_n^\alpha(x)\,P_k(x)\,w(x) = Q(x)\,\big(P_k(x)\big)^2\,(1 - x^2)^{\alpha - \frac{1}{2}}.
\]
For every $x \in (-1, 1)$:
- $w(x) = (1-x^2)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}} > 0$,
- $(P_k(x))^2 \ge 0$, vanishing only at the finitely many points $x_1, \ldots, x_k$,
- $Q(x)$ is strictly positive throughout $(-1,1)$ or strictly negative throughout $(-1,1)$.

Hence the integrand is continuous on $(-1,1)$, never changes sign on $(-1,1)$, and is strictly non-zero on $(-1,1)\setminus\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\}$ (in particular strictly non-zero on some non-empty closed subinterval of $(-1,1)$). Therefore
\[
I = \int_{-1}^{1} Q(x)\,\big(P_k(x)\big)^2\,w(x)\,dx \neq 0.
\]

**Step B: Show that $k < n$ implies $I = 0$, yielding a contradiction.**
Suppose for contradiction that $k < n$. For every integer $m \ge 0$, $C_m^\alpha(x)$ has exact degree $m$, so the polynomials $\{C_0^\alpha, C_1^\alpha, \ldots, C_k^\alpha\}$ are linearly independent and form a basis for the $(k+1)$-dimensional vector space of polynomials of degree at most $k$. (Explicitly, by downward induction on $m$, subtracting a suitable multiple of $C_m^\alpha$ cancels the $x^m$ term of any polynomial of degree $m$.)

Thus $P_k(x)$ can be expanded uniquely as a linear combination
\[
P_k(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{k} \beta_m C_m^\alpha(x)
\]
for some constants $\beta_0, \ldots, \beta_k$. Substituting into $I$ and using linearity gives
\[
I = \sum_{m=0}^{k} \beta_m \int_{-1}^{1} C_n^\alpha(x)\,C_m^\alpha(x)\,w(x)\,dx.
\]
Since $k < n$, every index $m \in \{0, 1, \ldots, k\}$ satisfies $m \neq n$, so by Part 1 every integral in the sum equals $0$. Hence $I = 0$, contradicting $I \neq 0$ from Step A.

Therefore $k < n$ is impossible. Since $k \le n$ (a degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n$ roots), we conclude that $k = n$: **all $n$ roots of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ are real, simple (distinct), and lie strictly inside $(-1, 1)$**.

## Final Answers

- **Interval endpoints:** $a = -1$, $b = 1$.
- **Weight function:** $w(x) = (1 - x^2)^{\alpha - \frac{1}{2}}$ (unique up to a non-zero constant factor), with Sturm–Liouville coefficient $p(x) = (1 - x^2)^{\alpha + \frac{1}{2}}$.
- **Roots:** For every $n \ge 0$ and $\alpha > 0$, all $n$ roots of $C_n^\alpha(x)$ are real, simple, and lie in $(-1, 1)$.

  - **Edge cases $n=0$ and $k=0$:** Check that $n=0$ (vacuous) and $k=0$ (empty product $P_0(x)=1$) are not broken by the notation.
