### Equivalence to a random walk on a graph with 6 edges

Consider an undirected multigraph $G=(V,E)$ with vertex set $V=\{a,b,c\}$ and no self-loops (since all diagonal entries of $P$ are $0$). Let $e_{uv}=e_{vu}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ denote the number of undirected edges between distinct vertices $u$ and $v$, and let $d_u = \sum_{v\ne u} e_{uv}$ be the degree of vertex $u$. In a simple random walk on $G$, at each step from vertex $u$ one of the $d_u$ incident edges is chosen uniformly at random, giving transition probabilities $p_{uv}=e_{uv}/d_u$ ($u\ne v$) and $p_{uu}=0$.

Take:
- $e_{ab} = 3$ parallel edges between $a$ and $b$,
- $e_{ac} = 2$ parallel edges between $a$ and $c$,
- $e_{bc} = 1$ edge between $b$ and $c$.

Then:
- The total number of edges is $|E| = e_{ab}+e_{ac}+e_{bc} = 3+2+1 = 6$.
- The vertex degrees are $d_a = 3+2 = 5$, $d_b = 3+1 = 4$, and $d_c = 2+1 = 3$ (with $\sum_{v\in V} d_v = 12 = 2|E|$).
- The transition probabilities of the random walk on $G$ are
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
  p_{ab}&=\frac{3}{5}, & p_{ac}&=\frac{2}{5}, & p_{aa}&=0,\\
  p_{ba}&=\frac{3}{4}, & p_{bc}&=\frac{1}{4}, & p_{bb}&=0,\\
  p_{ca}&=\frac{2}{3}, & p_{cb}&=\frac{1}{3}, & p_{cc}&=0,
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  which match $P$ identically.

(Note: Since a simple graph on $3$ vertices has at most $\binom{3}{2}=3$ edges, a graph on $\{a,b,c\}$ with $6$ edges is necessarily a multigraph, equivalently a weighted graph on $\{a,b,c\}$ with positive integer edge weights summing to $6$.)

Moreover, for any random walk on a connected finite multigraph $G=(V,E)$, the distribution $\pi_v = d_v/(2|E|)$ satisfies detailed balance:
\[
\pi_u p_{uv} = \frac{d_u}{2|E|}\cdot\frac{e_{uv}}{d_u} = \frac{e_{uv}}{2|E|} = \frac{d_v}{2|E|}\cdot\frac{e_{vu}}{d_v} = \pi_v p_{vu} \qquad \text{for all } u,v\in V.
\]
Here $\pi = (\pi_a,\pi_b,\pi_c) = \left(\frac{5}{12}, \frac{4}{12}, \frac{3}{12}\right)$ (matching $(\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3)=(5,4,3)/12$ in part (iii), identifying $1,2,3$ with $a,b,c$). Summing detailed balance over $u\in V$ shows $\sum_u \pi_u p_{uv} = \pi_v \sum_u p_{vu} = \pi_v$, so $\pi$ is an invariant distribution. Since all off-diagonal entries of $P$ are strictly positive, every pair of states communicates in $1$ step, so the chain is **irreducible**; since the state space is finite, the chain is **positive recurrent** and $\pi$ is its **unique** invariant distribution.

### Part (i): Find $k(a,a)$ and $k(a,b)$

Let $T_j = \inf\{n \ge 1 : X_n = j\}$ be the first passage time (first return time when starting at $j$) to state $j$, so that $k(i,j) = \mathbb{E}_i[T_j]$. (For $i \ne j$, $T_j$ coincides with the hitting time $H_j = \inf\{n \ge 0 : X_n = j\}$, whereas for $i = j$, $k(i,i) = \mathbb{E}_i[T_i]$ is the mean return time.)

**Finding $k(a,a)$:**
Because the chain is irreducible and positive recurrent with unique invariant distribution $\pi = (5/12, 4/12, 3/12)$, by the standard theorem relating the invariant distribution to mean return times,
\[
k(a,a) = \mathbb{E}_a[T_a] = \frac{1}{\pi_a} = \frac{1}{5/12} = \frac{12}{5}.
\]

*(Direct check by first-step analysis:* computing $k(b,a)$ and $k(c,a)$ below gives $k(b,a) = \frac{15}{11}$ and $k(c,a) = \frac{16}{11}$, and conditioning on $X_1$ gives $k(a,a) = 1 + \frac{3}{5}k(b,a) + \frac{2}{5}k(c,a) = 1 + \frac{9}{11} + \frac{32}{55} = \frac{132}{55} = \frac{12}{5}$.)

**Finding $k(a,b)$:**
For the hitting time $H_b = \inf\{n \ge 0 : X_n = b\}$, let $h_i = \mathbb{E}_i[H_b]$. By the standard IB theorem on mean hitting times, $(h_i)_{i\in\{a,b,c\}}$ is the minimal non-negative solution to the first-step equations:
\[
h_b = 0, \qquad h_i = 1 + \sum_{j\in\{a,b,c\}} p_{ij} h_j \quad (i \ne b).
\]
For $i \in \{a, c\}$ this gives:
\[
\begin{aligned}
k(a,b) &= 1 + \frac{3}{5}\cdot 0 + \frac{2}{5} k(c,b) = 1 + \frac{2}{5} k(c,b), \\
k(c,b) &= 1 + \frac{2}{3} k(a,b) + \frac{1}{3}\cdot 0 = 1 + \frac{2}{3} k(a,b).
\end{aligned}
\]
Substituting the second equation into the first:
\[
k(a,b) = 1 + \frac{2}{5}\left(1 + \frac{2}{3}k(a,b)\right) = \frac{7}{5} + \frac{4}{15}k(a,b)
\implies \frac{11}{15}k(a,b) = \frac{7}{5}
\implies k(a,b) = \frac{21}{11}.
\]
Then $k(c,b) = 1 + \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{21}{11} = \frac{25}{11}$. Since this $2\times 2$ linear system has a unique solution and both components are positive, it is automatically the minimal non-negative solution, so
\[
k(a,b) = \frac{21}{11}.
\]

### Part (ii): Expected number of steps from $X_0 = a$ until first completing a step from $b$ to $c$

Let $\sigma = \inf\{n \ge 1 : X_{n-1} = b \text{ and } X_n = c\}$, and let $g(i) = \mathbb{E}_i[\sigma]$ for $i \in \{a, b, c\}$. We seek $g(a)$.

We can represent $\sigma$ as the hitting time of an absorbing state $*$ in an augmented Markov chain on $\{a, b, c, *\}$ obtained from $P$ by redirecting the transition $b \to c$ (probability $1/4$) to $b \to *$ (probability $1/4$) and setting $p_{**}=1$. From every state in $\{a,b,c\}$, state $*$ is reachable in at most $2$ steps with probability at least $\min\left(\frac{3}{5}\cdot\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{12} > 0$, so $\mathbb{P}_i(\sigma > 2m) \le (11/12)^m$ and hence $g(i) < \infty$ for all $i\in\{a,b,c\}$.

By the first-step equations for expected hitting times (minimal non-negative solution; unique since the matrix $I - Q$ below is invertible):
\[
\begin{aligned}
g(a) &= 1 + \frac{3}{5} g(b) + \frac{2}{5} g(c), \\
g(b) &= 1 + \frac{3}{4} g(a) + \frac{1}{4}\cdot 0 = 1 + \frac{3}{4} g(a), \\
g(c) &= 1 + \frac{2}{3} g(a) + \frac{1}{3} g(b).
\end{aligned}
\]
Substitute $g(b) = 1 + \frac{3}{4}g(a)$ into the equation for $g(c)$:
\[
g(c) = 1 + \frac{2}{3}g(a) + \frac{1}{3}\left(1 + \frac{3}{4}g(a)\right) = \frac{4}{3} + \frac{11}{12}g(a).
\]
Substitute both $g(b)$ and $g(c)$ into the equation for $g(a)$:
\[
g(a) = 1 + \frac{3}{5}\left(1 + \frac{3}{4}g(a)\right) + \frac{2}{5}\left(\frac{4}{3} + \frac{11}{12}g(a)\right)
= \left(1 + \frac{3}{5} + \frac{8}{15}\right) + \left(\frac{9}{20} + \frac{11}{30}\right) g(a).
\]
Simplifying the coefficients:
\[
1 + \frac{3}{5} + \frac{8}{15} = \frac{32}{15}, \qquad \frac{9}{20} + \frac{11}{30} = \frac{27 + 22}{60} = \frac{49}{60}.
\]
Thus
\[
\frac{11}{60} g(a) = \frac{32}{15} \implies g(a) = \frac{32}{15}\cdot\frac{60}{11} = \frac{128}{11}.
\]
(This also yields $g(b) = \frac{107}{11}$ and $g(c) = 12$.)

*(Alternative slick derivation via strong Markov property:* to complete a step $b\to c$ starting from $a$, the chain must first hit $b$ (mean time $k(a,b) = 21/11$) and then, independently by the strong Markov property at $H_b$, complete a step $b\to c$ starting from $b$ (mean time $g(b)$), so $g(a) = k(a,b) + g(b)$. From $b$, with probability $1/4$ the walk steps to $c$ immediately (1 step), and with probability $3/4$ it steps to $a$ (1 step plus $g(a) = k(a,b) + g(b)$). Hence
\[
g(b) = 1 + \frac{3}{4}\big(k(a,b) + g(b)\big) \implies \frac{1}{4}g(b) = 1 + \frac{3}{4}k(a,b) \implies g(b) = 4 + 3k(a,b) = \frac{107}{11},
\]
and therefore $g(a) = k(a,b) + g(b) = 4 + 4k(a,b) = 4 + \frac{84}{11} = \frac{128}{11}$.)

### Part (iii): Reversibility, $E\tau(a,b)=E\tau(b,a)$, and the mean first passage time identity

Let $X_0 \sim \pi = (\pi_a, \pi_b, \pi_c) = (5,4,3)/12$.

**Step 1: Equality of distributions of $(X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_m)$ and $(X_m, \ldots, X_1, X_0)$.**
We already verified above that $\pi$ satisfies detailed balance $\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}$ for all $i,j \in \{a,b,c\}$:
\[
\pi_a p_{ab} = \frac{5}{12}\cdot\frac{3}{5} = \frac{1}{4} = \frac{4}{12}\cdot\frac{3}{4} = \pi_b p_{ba}, \quad
\pi_a p_{ac} = \frac{5}{12}\cdot\frac{2}{5} = \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{12}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \pi_c p_{ca}, \quad
\pi_b p_{bc} = \frac{4}{12}\cdot\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12} = \frac{3}{12}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \pi_c p_{cb},
\]
and trivially for $i=j$.

Fix any integer $m \ge 0$ and any sequence of states $(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_m) \in \{a,b,c\}^{m+1}$. By the Markov property and repeated application of detailed balance $\pi_{x_r} p_{x_r x_{r+1}} = \pi_{x_{r+1}} p_{x_{r+1} x_r}$ for $r = 0, 1, \ldots, m-1$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(X_0 = x_0, X_1 = x_1, \ldots, X_m = x_m)
&= \pi_{x_0} p_{x_0 x_1} p_{x_1 x_2} \cdots p_{x_{m-1} x_m} \\
&= p_{x_1 x_0} \pi_{x_1} p_{x_1 x_2} \cdots p_{x_{m-1} x_m} \\
&= \cdots \\
&= p_{x_1 x_0} p_{x_2 x_1} \cdots p_{x_m x_{m-1}} \pi_{x_m} \\
&= \pi_{x_m} p_{x_m x_{m-1}} \cdots p_{x_1 x_0} \\
&= \mathbb{P}(X_0 = x_m, X_1 = x_{m-1}, \ldots, X_m = x_0) \\
&= \mathbb{P}(X_m = x_0, X_{m-1} = x_1, \ldots, X_0 = x_m).
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence $(X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_m)$ and $(X_m, \ldots, X_1, X_0)$ have the same joint distribution for every $m \ge 0$.

**Step 2: Showing $E\tau(a,b) = E\tau(b,a)$.**
By definition, $\tau(a,b) \le m$ if and only if $(a,b)$ appears as a subsequence of $(X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_m)$, meaning there exist indices $0 \le r < s \le m$ such that $X_r = a$ and $X_s = b$.

Observe that for any deterministic sequence $(x_0, \ldots, x_m)$, the pair $(a,b)$ is a subsequence of $(x_0, \ldots, x_m)$ if and only if $(b,a)$ is a subsequence of the reversed sequence $(x_m, \ldots, x_0)$: indeed, $0 \le r < s \le m$ with $x_r = a, x_s = b$ corresponds bijectively to indices $r' = m - s$ and $s' = m - r$ satisfying $0 \le r' < s' \le m$ in the reversed sequence $y_k = x_{m-k}$ with $y_{r'} = x_s = b$ and $y_{s'} = x_r = a$.

Therefore, for every integer $m \ge 0$, using the equality in distribution from Step 1:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}\big(\tau(a,b) \le m\big)
&= \mathbb{P}\big((a,b) \text{ is a subsequence of } (X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_m)\big) \\
&= \mathbb{P}\big((b,a) \text{ is a subsequence of } (X_m, \ldots, X_1, X_0)\big) \\
&= \mathbb{P}\big((b,a) \text{ is a subsequence of } (X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_m)\big) \\
&= \mathbb{P}\big(\tau(b,a) \le m\big).
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently, $\mathbb{P}(\tau(a,b) > m) = \mathbb{P}(\tau(b,a) > m)$ for all $m \ge 0$ (so $\tau(a,b)$ and $\tau(b,a)$ are identically distributed). Since both are non-negative integer-valued random variables (with finite expectation, as shown below),
\[
E\tau(a,b) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}\big(\tau(a,b) > m\big) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}\big(\tau(b,a) > m\big) = E\tau(b,a).
\]

**Step 3: Deducing $k(b,a) - k(a,b) = \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i [k(i,a) - k(i,b)]$.**
Let $H_a = \inf\{n \ge 0 : X_n = a\}$ be the first hitting time of $a$. Since $a \ne b$, $(a,b)$ first appears as a subsequence of $(X_0, \ldots, X_m)$ at the first time after $H_a$ that the chain visits $b$:
\[
\tau(a,b) = H_a + \tilde{T}_b, \qquad \text{where } \tilde{T}_b = \inf\{r \ge 1 : X_{H_a + r} = b\}.
\]
Since the chain is finite and irreducible, $H_a < \infty$ almost surely (with finite expectation from every starting state). By the **strong Markov property** applied at the stopping time $H_a$ (where $X_{H_a} = a$), the post-$H_a$ chain $(X_{H_a + r})_{r \ge 0}$ is a Markov chain with transition matrix $P$ starting from $a$, independent of $(X_0, \ldots, X_{H_a})$ and in particular independent of $X_0$. Hence, for every $i \in \{a,b,c\}$,
\[
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{T}_b \mid X_0 = i] = \mathbb{E}_a[T_b] = k(a,b).
\]
Meanwhile, for $H_a$:
- if $i \ne a$, then $H_a = T_a = \inf\{n \ge 1 : X_n = a\}$, so $\mathbb{E}[H_a \mid X_0 = i] = k(i,a)$;
- if $i = a$, then $H_a = 0$, so $\mathbb{E}[H_a \mid X_0 = a] = 0$.

Conditioning on $X_0 \sim \pi$ by the law of total expectation gives:
\[
E\tau(a,b) = \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i \big(\mathbb{E}[H_a \mid X_0 = i] + k(a,b)\big) = k(a,b) + \sum_{i \ne a} \pi_i k(i,a) < \infty.
\]
Since the chain is irreducible and positive recurrent, $k(a,a) = 1/\pi_a$, so $\pi_a k(a,a) = 1$. Adding and subtracting $\pi_a k(a,a) = 1$ allows us to include $i = a$ in the sum:
\[
E\tau(a,b) = k(a,b) + \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i k(i,a) - \pi_a k(a,a) = k(a,b) + \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i k(i,a) - 1.
\]
By symmetry (interchanging $a$ and $b$, and using $\pi_b k(b,b) = 1$),
\[
E\tau(b,a) = k(b,a) + \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i k(i,b) - 1.
\]
Equating $E\tau(a,b) = E\tau(b,a)$, the $-1$ terms cancel, giving
\[
k(a,b) + \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i k(i,a) = k(b,a) + \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i k(i,b),
\]
and rearranging yields
\[
k(b,a) - k(a,b) = \sum_{i\in\{a,b,c\}} \pi_i \big[k(i,a) - k(i,b)\big],
\]
as required.
