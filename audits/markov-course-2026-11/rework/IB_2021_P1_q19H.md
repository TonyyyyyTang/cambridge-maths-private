## Full Solution

### Bookwork Definitions

Let $(X_n)_{n\geqslant 0}$ be a Markov chain on a countable state space $I$ with transition matrix $P$.

- **Stopping time:** A random variable $T : \Omega \to \{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\}$ is a *stopping time* of $(X_n)_{n\geqslant 0}$ if, for every integer $n \geqslant 0$, the event $\{T = n\}$ depends only on $(X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n)$ — that is, for each $n \geqslant 0$ there exists a subset $A_n \subseteq I^{n+1}$ such that
  \[
  \{T = n\} = \{(X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n) \in A_n\}.
  \]
  (Equivalently, $\{T \leqslant n\}$ depends only on $(X_0, \dots, X_n)$ for every $n \geqslant 0$, or $\{T = n\} \in \sigma(X_0, \dots, X_n)$.)

- **Strong Markov property:** Let $(X_n)_{n\geqslant 0}$ be $\operatorname{Markov}(\lambda, P)$ and let $T$ be a stopping time of $(X_n)_{n\geqslant 0}$. Then, for each $i \in I$ with $\mathbb{P}(T < \infty, X_T = i) > 0$, conditional on the event $\{T < \infty, X_T = i\}$, the post-$T$ process $(X_{T+n})_{n\geqslant 0}$ is $\operatorname{Markov}(\delta_i, P)$ and is independent of $(X_0, X_1, \dots, X_T)$ — i.e., for any $k \geqslant 0$, any $i_1, \dots, i_k \in I$, and any event $B$ determined by $(X_0, \dots, X_T)$ (meaning $B \cap \{T = n\}$ depends only on $X_0, \dots, X_n$ for all $n \geqslant 0$),
  \[
  \mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{T+1} = i_1, \dots, X_{T+k} = i_k \;\big|\; B, T < \infty, X_T = i\bigr) = P_{i, i_1} P_{i_1, i_2} \cdots P_{i_{k-1}, i_k}
  \]
  whenever $\mathbb{P}(B, T < \infty, X_T = i) > 0$.

### Setting up the Markov Chain

Let the state space be $S = \{1, 2, 4\} \times \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, where $X_n = (s, k) \in S$ records the player's square $s$ and wealth £$k$ after $n$ turns (having already applied any immediate square-3 penalty and return to square 2, so the player is never on square 3 at the start or end of a turn).

Because coin tosses are fair (probability $1/2$ each for Heads = move $+1$ and Tails = move $+2$) and independent of the past, $(X_n)_{n\geqslant 0}$ is a Markov chain on $S$. Its transition probabilities are:
1. **Absorbing losing state $(2, 0)$:** The game ends when the player is at square 2 with £0, so
   \[
   p_{(2,0),(2,0)} = 1.
   \]
2. **From $(2, k)$ with $k \geqslant 1$:**
   - Heads ($+1$): lands on square 3 $\implies$ immediately loses £1 and returns to square 2 $\implies$ moves to $(2, k-1)$ with probability $1/2$.
   - Tails ($+2$): moves $2 \to 3 \to 4$, passing over square 3 without landing on it and not passing square 1 $\implies$ moves to $(4, k)$ with probability $1/2$.
3. **From $(4, k)$ with $k \geqslant 0$:**
   - Heads ($+1$): lands on square 1, collects £2 $\implies$ moves to $(1, k+2)$ with probability $1/2$.
   - Tails ($+2$): passes square 1 and lands on square 2, collects £2 $\implies$ moves to $(2, k+2)$ with probability $1/2$.
4. **From $(1, k)$ with $k \geqslant 1$:**
   - Heads ($+1$): lands on square 2 $\implies$ moves to $(2, k)$ with probability $1/2$.
   - Tails ($+2$): lands on square 3 $\implies$ immediately loses £1 and returns to square 2 $\implies$ moves to $(2, k-1)$ with probability $1/2$.
5. *(Boundary state $(1, 0)$)*: Notice that starting from any state $(s, m)$ with $m \geqslant 1$, wealth can only decrease by $1$ upon arriving at square 2, and increases by $2$ upon arriving at square 1 (from $(4, k)$ to $(1, k+2)$). Thus $(1, 0)$ is never reached from any state with $k \geqslant 1$ (nor from $(4,0)$ or $(2,0)$). For completeness so that $P$ is a stochastic matrix on all of $S$, we may set $p_{(1,0),(1,0)} = 1$.

### Part (a)

For each state $x = (s, k) \in S$, let $H_y = \inf\{n \geqslant 0 : X_n = y\}$ be the hitting time of $y \in S$, and let
\[
h(s, k) = \mathbb{P}_{(s, k)}(H_{(2, 0)} < \infty)
\]
be the probability of ever reaching $(2, 0)$ starting from $(s, k)$. Also, for $m \geqslant 1$, let
\[
\alpha_m = \mathbb{P}_{(2, m)}\bigl(H_{(2, m-1)} < \infty\bigr).
\]

**Step 1: $\alpha_m$ is independent of $m \geqslant 1$, and downward steps factor by the strong Markov property.**
Observe that the ONLY transitions in which wealth decreases are $(2, k) \to (2, k-1)$ and $(1, k) \to (2, k-1)$ (both for $k \geqslant 1$). In every single transition:
- wealth decreases by at most $1$, and
- whenever wealth decreases by $1$, the chain lands on square $2$.

Consequently, starting from $(2, m)$ ($m \geqslant 1$), up to the stopping time $H_{(2, m-1)}$, the chain only ever visits states $(s, k)$ with $k \geqslant m$ (and hits $(2, m-1)$ as its very first state with wealth $< m$). On the set $\{(s, k) : k \geqslant m\}$, all transition probabilities depend only on the square $s$ and the relative change in wealth, never on the absolute wealth level (and the absorbing state $(2,0)$ is not encountered prior to $H_{(2, m-1)}$). Shifting the wealth coordinate by $c \geqslant 0$ gives a measure-preserving bijection between paths from $(2, 1)$ hitting $(2, 0)$ and paths from $(2, 1+c)$ hitting $(2, c)$. Therefore
\[
\alpha_m = \alpha_1 =: \alpha \qquad \text{for all } m \geqslant 1.
\]
Moreover, for any $r > \ell \geqslant 0$, any trajectory starting at $(2, r)$ that eventually reaches $(2, \ell)$ must first visit $(2, r-1)$ at the stopping time $T = H_{(2, r-1)} < \infty$ (since wealth starts at $r$, drops by at most $1$ per step, and can only drop upon landing at square 2). Applying the **strong Markov property** at the stopping time $T = H_{(2, r-1)}$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}_{(2, r)}(H_{(2, \ell)} < \infty)
&= \mathbb{P}_{(2, r)}\bigl(T < \infty \text{ and } \exists n \geqslant 0 : X_{T+n} = (2, \ell)\bigr) \\
&= \mathbb{P}_{(2, r)}(T < \infty)\,\mathbb{P}_{(2, r-1)}(H_{(2, \ell)} < \infty) \\
&= \alpha\,\mathbb{P}_{(2, r-1)}(H_{(2, \ell)} < \infty).
\end{aligned}
\]
By induction on $r - \ell \geqslant 0$, we obtain
\[
\mathbb{P}_{(2, r)}(H_{(2, \ell)} < \infty) = \alpha^{r - \ell} \qquad \text{for all } r \geqslant \ell \geqslant 0.
\]
In particular, taking $\ell = 0$, $h(2, k) = \alpha^k$ for all $k \geqslant 0$.

**Step 2: Equation for $\alpha$.**
Starting from $(2, m)$ with $m \geqslant 1$, trace all trajectories until the first return to square $2$ (the stopping time $\tau = \inf\{n \geqslant 1 : X_n \in \{2\} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\}$):
- With probability $1/2$: $(2, m) \to (2, m-1)$ (in $1$ step).
- With probability $1/2$: $(2, m) \to (4, m)$. From $(4, m)$:
  - With probability $1/2$: $(4, m) \to (2, m+2)$ (net prob $1/4$).
  - With probability $1/2$: $(4, m) \to (1, m+2)$, from which:
    - With probability $1/2$: $(1, m+2) \to (2, m+2)$ (net prob $1/8$),
    - With probability $1/2$: $(1, m+2) \to (2, m+1)$ (net prob $1/8$).

Notice that before $\tau$, the chain never visits $(2, m-1)$ (the intermediate states are $(4, m)$ and $(1, m+2)$), and $\tau \leqslant 3$ almost surely. Thus $X_\tau$ takes values:
- $(2, m-1)$ with probability $1/2$,
- $(2, m+1)$ with probability $1/8$,
- $(2, m+2)$ with probability $1/4 + 1/8 = 3/8$.

Applying the strong Markov property at $\tau$ (or equivalently applying the Markov property step-by-step at times $1, 2, 3$):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha = \mathbb{P}_{(2, m)}(H_{(2, m-1)} < \infty)
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{8}\,\mathbb{P}_{(2, m+1)}(H_{(2, m-1)} < \infty) + \frac{3}{8}\,\mathbb{P}_{(2, m+2)}(H_{(2, m-1)} < \infty) \\
&= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{8}\alpha^2 + \frac{3}{8}\alpha^3.
\end{aligned}
\]
Rearranging gives the cubic equation
\[
3\alpha^3 + \alpha^2 - 8\alpha + 4 = 0.
\]
Factoring out $(\alpha - 1)$:
\[
3\alpha^3 + \alpha^2 - 8\alpha + 4 = (\alpha - 1)(3\alpha^2 + 4\alpha - 4) = (\alpha - 1)(3\alpha - 2)(\alpha + 2) = 0.
\]
The roots are $\alpha = -2$, $\alpha = 2/3$, and $\alpha = 1$. Since $\alpha$ is a probability, $\alpha \in [0, 1]$, so either $\alpha = 2/3$ or $\alpha = 1$.

**Step 3: Ruling out $\alpha = 1$ via minimality of hitting probabilities.**
Recall (and we prove briefly below) that the hitting probability vector $h(x) = \mathbb{P}_x(H_{(2,0)} < \infty)$ is the minimal non-negative solution to the system
\[
g(2, 0) = 1, \qquad g(x) = \sum_{y \in S} p_{xy} g(y) \quad \text{for all } x \neq (2, 0).
\]
*(Self-contained proof of minimality: Let $g : S \to [0, \infty)$ satisfy $g(2,0) = 1$ and $g(x) = \sum_y p_{xy} g(y)$ for all $x \neq (2,0)$. We show by induction on $n \geqslant 0$ that $g(x) \geqslant \mathbb{P}_x(H_{(2,0)} \leqslant n)$ for all $x \in S$. For $n=0$, $\mathbb{P}_x(H_{(2,0)} \leqslant 0) = \mathbf{1}_{\{x=(2,0)\}} \leqslant g(x)$ since $g \geqslant 0$ and $g(2,0) = 1$. Assuming the inequality for $n$, for $x = (2,0)$ we have $g(2,0) = 1 = \mathbb{P}_{(2,0)}(H_{(2,0)} \leqslant n+1)$, while for $x \neq (2,0)$,*
\[
\mathbb{P}_x(H_{(2,0)} \leqslant n+1) = \sum_{y \in S} p_{xy}\,\mathbb{P}_y(H_{(2,0)} \leqslant n) \leqslant \sum_{y \in S} p_{xy} g(y) = g(x).
\]
*Letting $n \to \infty$ by continuity of probability gives $h(x) = \mathbb{P}_x(H_{(2,0)} < \infty) \leqslant g(x)$ for all $x \in S$.)*

Now define $g : S \to [0, \infty)$ by
\[
g(2, k) = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k \ (k \geqslant 0), \qquad
g(4, k) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k \ (k \geqslant 0), \qquad
g(1, k) = \begin{cases} \frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k & (k \geqslant 1), \\ 0 & (k = 0). \end{cases}
\]
Clearly $g(x) \geqslant 0$ for all $x \in S$ and $g(2, 0) = 1$. Checking $(Pg)(x) = g(x)$ for all $x \neq (2, 0)$:
- For $x = (1, 0)$: $(Pg)(1,0) = g(1,0) = 0$.
- For $x = (1, k)$ ($k \geqslant 1$):
  \[
  (Pg)(1, k) = \frac{1}{2}g(2, k) + \frac{1}{2}g(2, k-1) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k = \frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k = g(1, k).
  \]
- For $x = (2, k)$ ($k \geqslant 1$):
  \[
  (Pg)(2, k) = \frac{1}{2}g(2, k-1) + \frac{1}{2}g(4, k) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k + \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k = g(2, k).
  \]
- For $x = (4, k)$ ($k \geqslant 0$):
  \[
  (Pg)(4, k) = \frac{1}{2}g(1, k+2) + \frac{1}{2}g(2, k+2) = \left(\frac{5}{8} + \frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k+2} = \frac{9}{8}\cdot\frac{4}{9}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k = g(4, k).
  \]

By minimality, $h(x) \leqslant g(x)$ for all $x \in S$. In particular,
\[
\alpha = h(2, 1) \leqslant g(2, 1) = \frac{2}{3} < 1.
\]
Hence $\alpha \neq 1$, and therefore $\alpha = 2/3$. Thus, for every $m \geqslant 1$, the probability that a player at $(2, m)$ ever reaches $(2, m-1)$ is **$2/3$**.

### Part (b)

We want $h(1, m) = \mathbb{P}_{(1, m)}(H_{(2, 0)} < \infty)$ for $m \geqslant 1$.

From Step 1 and Part (a), we already know that for every $k \geqslant 0$,
\[
h(2, k) = \alpha^k = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k.
\]
Starting from $(1, m)$ with $m \geqslant 1$, condition on the first coin toss (Markov property at $n = 1$):
- With probability $1/2$ (Heads), the player moves to $(2, m)$, from which the probability of losing is $h(2, m)$.
- With probability $1/2$ (Tails), the player lands on square 3, immediately loses £1 and returns to square 2, reaching $(2, m-1)$, from which the probability of losing is $h(2, m-1)$.

Therefore, for all $m \geqslant 1$,
\[
h(1, m) = \frac{1}{2} h(2, m) + \frac{1}{2} h(2, m-1)
= \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^m + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{m-1}
= \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3} + 1\right)\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{m-1}
= \frac{5}{6}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{m-1} = \frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^m.
\]
*(Sanity check at $m = 1$: from $(1, 1)$, Tails immediately loses (prob $1/2$) and Heads goes to $(2, 1)$ (prob $1/2$, then loses with prob $2/3$), giving $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \frac{5}{6}$, matching $\frac{5}{6}(2/3)^0 = \frac{5}{6}$.)*

## Final Answers

- **Bookwork**: Stopping time ($\{T=n\}$ depends only on $X_0,\dots,X_n$ for all $n\geqslant 0$) and Strong Markov property (conditional on $\{T<\infty, X_T=i\}$, $(X_{T+n})_{n\geqslant 0}$ is $\operatorname{Markov}(\delta_i, P)$ independent of $(X_0,\dots,X_T)$).
- **(a)**: $\mathbb{P}_{(2,m)}(\text{ever visit }(2, m-1)) = \dfrac{2}{3}$ for all $m \geqslant 1$.
- **(b)**: $\mathbb{P}_{(1,m)}(\text{lose}) = \dfrac{5}{6}\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^{m-1} = \dfrac{5}{4}\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^m$ for all $m \geqslant 1$.
