## Full Solution

### 1. Definitions

Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain with state space $I$ and transition matrix $P = (p_{ij})_{i,j \in I}$.

- For states $i, j \in I$, we say *$i$ leads to $j$*, written $i \to j$, if $\mathbb{P}_i(X_n = j \text{ for some } n \ge 0) > 0$; equivalently, there exists an integer $n \ge 0$ such that $p_{ij}^{(n)} > 0$ (with $P^0 = I$, so $i \to i$ holds for every $i \in I$).
- We say *$i$ communicates with $j$*, written $i \leftrightarrow j$, if $i \to j$ and $j \to i$.
- Since $\leftrightarrow$ is an equivalence relation on $I$ (reflexive since $p_{ii}^{(0)} = 1 > 0$; symmetric by definition; transitive since $p_{ij}^{(m)} > 0$ and $p_{jk}^{(n)} > 0$ imply $p_{ik}^{(m+n)} \ge p_{ij}^{(m)} p_{jk}^{(n)} > 0$), it partitions $I$ into equivalence classes. A subset $C \subseteq I$ is a **communicating class** if it is an equivalence class of $\leftrightarrow$ (equivalently, $C$ is non-empty, $i \leftrightarrow j$ for all $i, j \in C$, and whenever $i \in C$ and $i \leftrightarrow j$ we have $j \in C$).
- A subset $C \subseteq I$ is **closed** if for all $i \in C$ and $j \in I$,
  \[
  i \to j \implies j \in C
  \]
  (equivalently, $p_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \in C$ and $j \notin C$, i.e., $\sum_{j \in C} p_{ij} = 1$ for all $i \in C$: once the chain enters $C$, it cannot leave).

### 2. Communicating Classes and Closedness

The transition matrix on $I = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ is
\[
P = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{6} & 0 & \frac{1}{6} \\
\frac{1}{3} & \frac{2}{3} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & 0 & \frac{1}{3} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{2}{3} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\
\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
(Every row is non-negative and sums to $1$.) The positive one-step transitions are:
- $1 \to 2, 3, 4, 6$;
- $2 \to 1, 2$;
- $3 \to 2, 3, 5$;
- $4 \to 4, 5$;
- $5 \to 4, 5$;
- $6 \to 1, 3$.

Inspecting mutual reachability:
- **$\{4, 5\}$:** We have $p_{45} = \frac{2}{3} > 0$ and $p_{54} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$, so $4 \leftrightarrow 5$. Moreover, $p_{4j} = 0$ and $p_{5j} = 0$ for all $j \in \{1, 2, 3, 6\}$, so no state in $\{4, 5\}$ leads to any state outside $\{4, 5\}$. Thus $\{4, 5\}$ cannot communicate with any state outside $\{4, 5\}$, so **$\{4, 5\}$ is a communicating class and is closed**.
- **$\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$:**
  - $p_{12} = \frac{1}{3} > 0$ and $p_{21} = \frac{1}{3} > 0$, so $1 \leftrightarrow 2$;
  - $p_{13} = \frac{1}{3} > 0$, and $p_{32} p_{21} = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{9} > 0$ (path $3 \to 2 \to 1$), so $1 \leftrightarrow 3$;
  - $p_{16} = \frac{1}{6} > 0$ and $p_{61} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$, so $1 \leftrightarrow 6$.
  By transitivity of $\leftrightarrow$, all pairs of states in $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ communicate. Since $\{4, 5\}$ is closed, neither $4$ nor $5$ leads to any state in $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$. Hence **$\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ is a communicating class**.
  It is **not closed**, because $p_{14} = \frac{1}{6} > 0$ (and also $p_{35} = \frac{1}{3} > 0$), so the chain can leave $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ for $\{4, 5\}$.

Thus the communicating classes are **$\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ (not closed)** and **$\{4, 5\}$ (closed)**.

### 3. Probability of Ever Reaching $2$ Starting from $1$

Let $H_2 = \inf\{n \ge 0 : X_n = 2\}$ (with $\inf\emptyset = \infty$), and let
\[
h_i = \mathbb{P}_i(H_2 < \infty) = \mathbb{P}(X_n = 2 \text{ for some } n \ge 0 \mid X_0 = i), \qquad i \in \{1, \ldots, 6\}.
\]
(Note that since $X_0 = 1 \ne 2$, $\{X_n = 2 \text{ for some } n \ge 0\} = \{X_n = 2 \text{ for some } n \ge 1\}$ under $\mathbb{P}_1$.) We wish to compute $h_1$.

By the theorem on hitting probabilities, the vector $(h_1, \ldots, h_6)$ is the **minimal non-negative solution** to the first-step equations
\[
h_2 = 1, \qquad h_i = \sum_{j=1}^{6} p_{ij} h_j \quad \text{for } i \in \{1, 3, 4, 5, 6\}.
\]
Writing these out explicitly:
\begin{align*}
h_1 &= \frac{1}{3} h_2 + \frac{1}{3} h_3 + \frac{1}{6} h_4 + \frac{1}{6} h_6, \\
h_2 &= 1, \\
h_3 &= \frac{1}{3} h_2 + \frac{1}{3} h_3 + \frac{1}{3} h_5, \\
h_4 &= \frac{1}{3} h_4 + \frac{2}{3} h_5, \\
h_5 &= \frac{1}{2} h_4 + \frac{1}{2} h_5, \\
h_6 &= \frac{1}{2} h_1 + \frac{1}{2} h_3.
\end{align*}

First, since $\{4, 5\}$ is a closed communicating class and $2 \notin \{4, 5\}$, a chain starting in $4$ or $5$ remains in $\{4, 5\}$ forever with probability $1$, so it can never reach $2$; hence
\[
h_4 = h_5 = 0.
\]
*(This also follows directly from minimality: the equations for $h_4$ and $h_5$ both reduce to $h_4 = h_5 = c \ge 0$. Solving the remaining equations in terms of $c$ gives $h_3 = \frac{1+c}{2}$, $h_6 = \frac{1}{2}h_1 + \frac{1+c}{4}$, and $h_1 = \frac{13 + 9c}{22}$, all of which are strictly increasing in $c \ge 0$; thus the unique minimal non-negative solution occurs at $c = 0$.)*

Setting $h_2 = 1$ and $h_4 = h_5 = 0$:
1. From the $h_3$ equation:
   \[
   h_3 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} h_3 \implies \frac{2}{3} h_3 = \frac{1}{3} \implies h_3 = \frac{1}{2}.
   \]
2. From the $h_6$ equation:
   \[
   h_6 = \frac{1}{2} h_1 + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} h_1 + \frac{1}{4}.
   \]
3. Substituting $h_2 = 1$, $h_3 = \frac{1}{2}$, $h_4 = 0$, and $h_6 = \frac{1}{2} h_1 + \frac{1}{4}$ into the $h_1$ equation:
   \[
   h_1 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6}\!\left(\frac{1}{2} h_1 + \frac{1}{4}\right)
   = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{12} h_1 + \frac{1}{24}
   = \frac{13}{24} + \frac{1}{12} h_1.
   \]
   Therefore,
   \[
   \frac{11}{12} h_1 = \frac{13}{24} \implies h_1 = \frac{13}{24} \cdot \frac{12}{11} = \frac{13}{22},
   \]
   and $h_6 = \frac{13}{44} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{24}{44} = \frac{6}{11}$.

Hence the probability that the chain ever reaches $2$, given that it starts in $1$, is $\dfrac{13}{22}$.

## Final Answers

- **Communicating classes:** $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ and $\{4, 5\}$.
- **Closed classes:** $\{4, 5\}$ is closed; $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ is not closed.
- **Probability of ever reaching $2$ starting from $1$:** $\dfrac{13}{22}$ (with full hitting vector $(h_1, h_2, h_3, h_4, h_5, h_6) = \left(\frac{13}{22}, 1, \frac{1}{2}, 0, 0, \frac{6}{11}\right)$).
