## Full Solution

### Part 1: Detailed balance implies invariance

Let $S$ be a countable state space, $P = (p_{ij})_{i,j\in S}$ be a stochastic transition matrix (so $p_{ij} \ge 0$ for all $i,j \in S$ and $\sum_{j \in S} p_{ij} = 1$ for all $i \in S$), and $\pi = (\pi_i)_{i\in S}$ be a distribution on $S$ (so $\pi_i \ge 0$ for all $i \in S$ and $\sum_{i \in S} \pi_i = 1$).

By definition, $\pi$ is in **detailed balance** with $P$ if
\[
\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji} \qquad \text{for all } i, j \in S.
\]
Summing this identity over all $i \in S$ (valid even for countably infinite $S$ since all terms are non-negative), we obtain for every $j \in S$:
\[
(\pi P)_j = \sum_{i \in S} \pi_i p_{ij} = \sum_{i \in S} \pi_j p_{ji} = \pi_j \sum_{i \in S} p_{ji} = \pi_j \cdot 1 = \pi_j,
\]
where the penultimate equality uses that the rows of $P$ sum to $1$. Hence $\pi P = \pi$. Since $\pi$ is already a distribution, $\pi$ is an **invariant distribution** for $P$.

*(Remark: The identical calculation with any non-negative measure $\lambda = (\lambda_i)_{i\in S}$ satisfying $\lambda_i p_{ij} = \lambda_j p_{ji}$ for all $i,j\in S$ shows that $\lambda P = \lambda$, i.e., $\lambda$ is an invariant measure for $P$.)*

### Part 2: Transition probabilities and invariant measure $\lambda_{i,j}$ for the 2-urn model

The state space is $S = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2 = \{(i,j) : i, j \in \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}\}$, where $i$ and $j$ are the numbers of balls in urn 1 and urn 2 respectively. The parameters satisfy $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \ge 0$, $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$, and $\alpha < \gamma$ (which implies $\gamma > 0$, so $\alpha/(2\gamma)$ is well-defined).

Let $n = i + j$ be the total number of balls in state $(i,j)$. When $n \ge 1$, choosing a ball uniformly at random from the $n$ balls selects a ball from urn 1 with probability $\frac{i}{i+j}$ and from urn 2 with probability $\frac{j}{i+j}$. Adding a ball to a randomly chosen urn selects urn 1 or urn 2 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each.

Hence the non-zero transition probabilities $p_{(i,j),(i',j')}$ are:
- From $(0,0)$:
  \[
  p_{(0,0),(0,0)} = \beta + \gamma, \qquad p_{(0,0),(1,0)} = p_{(0,0),(0,1)} = \frac{\alpha}{2}.
  \]
- From $(i,j)$ with $i + j \ge 1$:
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
  p_{(i,j),(i+1,j)} &= \frac{\alpha}{2}, & p_{(i,j),(i,j+1)} &= \frac{\alpha}{2}, \\
  p_{(i,j),(i-1,j)} &= \gamma\frac{i}{i+j} \quad (i \ge 1), & p_{(i,j),(i,j-1)} &= \gamma\frac{j}{i+j} \quad (j \ge 1), \\
  p_{(i,j),(i-1,j+1)} &= \beta\frac{i}{i+j} \quad (i \ge 1), & p_{(i,j),(i+1,j-1)} &= \beta\frac{j}{i+j} \quad (j \ge 1),
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  and $p_{(i,j),(i',j')} = 0$ for all other $(i',j')$ (note $p_{(i,j),(i,j)}=0$ for $i+j \ge 1$).

(Quick check of row sums: at $(0,0)$ we get $(\beta+\gamma)+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}=1$; at $(i,j)$ with $i+j\ge 1$ we get $\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\gamma\frac{i+j}{i+j}+\beta\frac{i+j}{i+j} = \alpha+\gamma+\beta = 1$.)

Now consider the non-negative measure
\[
\lambda_{i,j} = \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j}, \qquad (i,j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2
\]
(using the standard convention $0^0 = 1$ if $\alpha = 0$, in which case $\lambda_{0,0}=1$ and $\lambda_{i,j}=0$ for $i+j\ge 1$). We verify that $\lambda$ is in **detailed balance** with $P$, checking all pairs of distinct states with possible transitions between them:

1. **Addition/removal in urn 1**: between $(i,j)$ and $(i+1,j)$ for any $i,j \ge 0$:
   \[
   \lambda_{i,j}\, p_{(i,j),(i+1,j)}
   = \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{2}
   = \gamma \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j+1},
   \]
   and
   \[
   \lambda_{i+1,j}\, p_{(i+1,j),(i,j)}
   = \frac{(i+j+1)!}{(i+1)!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j+1} \cdot \gamma\frac{i+1}{i+j+1}
   = \gamma \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j+1}.
   \]
   These are equal!

2. **Addition/removal in urn 2**: between $(i,j)$ and $(i,j+1)$ for any $i,j \ge 0$. By symmetry between $i$ and $j$,
   \[
   \lambda_{i,j}\, p_{(i,j),(i,j+1)}
   = \gamma \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j+1}
   = \frac{(i+j+1)!}{i!(j+1)!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j+1} \cdot \gamma\frac{j+1}{i+j+1}
   = \lambda_{i,j+1}\, p_{(i,j+1),(i,j)}.
   \]

3. **Transfer between urns**: between $(i,j)$ and $(i-1,j+1)$ for any $i \ge 1, j \ge 0$:
   \[
   \lambda_{i,j}\, p_{(i,j),(i-1,j+1)}
   = \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j} \cdot \beta\frac{i}{i+j}
   = \beta \frac{(i+j-1)!}{(i-1)!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j},
   \]
   and
   \[
   \lambda_{i-1,j+1}\, p_{(i-1,j+1),(i,j)}
   = \frac{(i+j)!}{(i-1)!(j+1)!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j} \cdot \beta\frac{j+1}{i+j}
   = \beta \frac{(i+j-1)!}{(i-1)!j!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j}.
   \]
   These are equal! (And this covers transfers $(i,j) \leftrightarrow (i+1,j-1)$ for $j \ge 1$ by replacing $(i,j)$ with $(i+1,j-1)$.)

4. **Self-transitions and all other pairs**: For $(i',j') = (i,j)$, detailed balance $\lambda_{i,j} p_{(i,j),(i,j)} = \lambda_{i,j} p_{(i,j),(i,j)}$ holds trivially. For any other pair of states, both transition probabilities are $0$, so detailed balance holds as $0 = 0$.

Thus $\lambda$ is in detailed balance with $P$. Moreover, let us compute its total mass $Z = \sum_{i=0}^\infty \sum_{j=0}^\infty \lambda_{i,j}$. Since all $\lambda_{i,j} \ge 0$, we may group terms by the total number of balls $n = i+j$:
\[
\sum_{i+j=n} \lambda_{i,j}
= \sum_{i=0}^{n} \binom{n}{i}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^n
= 2^n \left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^n
= \left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^n.
\]
Since $0 \le \alpha < \gamma$, we have $0 \le \alpha/\gamma < 1$, so the geometric series converges:
\[
Z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^n = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha/\gamma} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma - \alpha} < \infty.
\]
Hence $\pi_{i,j} = \lambda_{i,j}/Z$ is a probability distribution in detailed balance with $P$. By Part 1, $\pi$ is an invariant distribution for $P$ ($\pi P = \pi$), and multiplying by $Z$ gives $\lambda P = \lambda$, proving that $\lambda$ is an invariant measure for $P$. (Equivalently, summing the detailed balance equations directly for $\lambda$ gives $(\lambda P)_{k,l} = \lambda_{k,l}$.)

### Part 3: Proportion of time for which there are $n$ balls in the system

From the calculation above, the normalized invariant distribution is
\[
\pi_{i,j} = \frac{\lambda_{i,j}}{Z} = \left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right) \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!} \left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^{i+j}, \qquad (i,j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2.
\]
Let $(I_t, J_t)$ denote the state at time $t$, and consider the long-run proportion of time for which $I_t + J_t = n$:
\[
\lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbf{1}_{\{I_t + J_t = n\}}.
\]

**Main case ($\alpha > 0$, with $\gamma > \alpha > 0$ and $\beta \ge 0$):**
- **Irreducibility**: From any state $(i,j)$, the chain can reach $(0,0)$ in $i+j$ steps by removing one ball at each step (each step having positive probability $\ge \gamma/(i+j) > 0$, since $\gamma > 0$). Conversely, from $(0,0)$ the chain can reach any state $(i',j')$ in $i'+j'$ steps by adding $i'$ balls to urn 1 and $j'$ balls to urn 2 (each step having probability $\alpha/2 > 0$). Hence every state communicates with $(0,0)$, so the chain is **irreducible** (even if $\beta = 0$).
- **Positive recurrence and uniqueness**: Because the chain is irreducible and admits the invariant distribution $\pi$, every state is positive recurrent and $\pi$ is the unique invariant distribution.
- **Ergodic Theorem**: By the Ergodic Theorem for irreducible positive recurrent Markov chains, for every state $(i,j)$ and any initial distribution,
  \[
  \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbf{1}_{\{(I_t, J_t) = (i,j)\}} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \pi_{i,j} \qquad \text{as } T \to \infty.
  \]
  For any fixed $n \in \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$, the set of states with $n$ balls, $A_n = \{(i, n-i) : i = 0, 1, \ldots, n\}$, is finite (of size $n+1$). Summing the $n+1$ limits gives:
  \[
  \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbf{1}_{\{I_t + J_t = n\}}
  = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbf{1}_{\{(I_t, J_t) = (i, n-i)\}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \sum_{i=0}^{n} \pi_{i, n-i}
  = \left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right) \sum_{i=0}^{n} \binom{n}{i}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}\right)^n
  = \left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^n.
  \]
  (Note also that the chain is aperiodic since $p_{(0,0),(0,0)} = \beta + \gamma > 0$, so $\mathbb{P}(I_t + J_t = n)$ also converges to $\left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^n$ as $t \to \infty$.)

**Boundary case ($\alpha = 0$, so $\gamma > 0$):**
If $\alpha = 0$, no balls are ever added. State $(0,0)$ is absorbing ($p_{(0,0),(0,0)} = \beta + \gamma = 1$). From any initial state with $n_0 = I_0 + J_0 \ge 1$ balls, the total number of balls $N_t = I_t + J_t$ decreases by $1$ with probability $\gamma > 0$ and stays unchanged with probability $\beta = 1 - \gamma$ at each step until reaching $0$. Thus $N_t$ hits $0$ in an almost surely finite time (sum of $n_0$ i.i.d. $\mathrm{Geom}(\gamma)$ random variables) and remains $0$ thereafter. Hence the long-run proportion of time with $n$ balls is $1$ for $n=0$ and $0$ for $n \ge 1$, which agrees with $\left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^n$ under the convention $0^0 = 1$.

## Final Answers

- Detailed balance $\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}$ implies $(\pi P)_j = \sum_i \pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j \sum_i p_{ji} = \pi_j$, so $\pi$ is invariant.
- $\lambda_{i,j} = \frac{(i+j)!}{i!j!}(\alpha/2\gamma)^{i+j}$ satisfies detailed balance with the 2-urn transition matrix $P$ and is therefore an invariant measure (with total mass $Z = (1 - \alpha/\gamma)^{-1} = \gamma/(\gamma-\alpha)$).
- The long-run proportion of time for which there are $n$ balls in the system ($n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$) is
  \[
  \left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^n
  \]
  (almost surely, independent of $\beta$ and of the initial state; valid also at $\alpha = 0$ with $0^0 = 1$).
