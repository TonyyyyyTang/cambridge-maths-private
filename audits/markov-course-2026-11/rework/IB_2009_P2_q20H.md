## Full Solution

Throughout, we consider real-valued functions $g : I \to \mathbb{R}$ on the countable state space $I$. Note at the outset that condition (a),
$$g_i \geqslant (Pg)_i \equiv \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} g_j \geqslant 0 \qquad \forall i \in I,$$
implies in particular that $g_i \geqslant 0$ for all $i \in I$ (by transitivity of $\geqslant$).

---

### Part 1: $g = h$ satisfies (a) and (b)

We are given $B \subseteq I$ non-empty, $\tau = \inf\{n \geqslant 0 : X_n \in B\}$ (with $\inf \emptyset = \infty$), and $h_i = \mathbb{P}(\tau < \infty \mid X_0 = i)$.

- **Condition (b):** If $i \in B$, then on $\{X_0 = i\}$ we have $X_0 \in B$, so $\tau = 0 < \infty$ surely. Hence
  $$h_i = \mathbb{P}(\tau < \infty \mid X_0 = i) = 1 \qquad \forall i \in B.$$

- **Condition (a):** Since $h_j$ is a probability, $0 \leqslant h_j \leqslant 1$ for all $j \in I$. Because $p_{ij} \geqslant 0$ and $\sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} = 1$, the series $(Ph)_i = \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} h_j$ converges and satisfies
  $$0 \leqslant (Ph)_i = \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} h_j \leqslant \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} \cdot 1 = 1 \qquad \forall i \in I.$$
  We check $h_i \geqslant (Ph)_i$ in two cases:
  1. **If $i \in B$:** then $h_i = 1 \geqslant (Ph)_i \geqslant 0$.
  2. **If $i \notin B$:** then on $\{X_0 = i\}$ we have $X_0 \notin B$, so $\tau = 1 + \tilde{\tau}$ where $\tilde{\tau} = \inf\{m \geqslant 0 : X_{m+1} \in B\}$. Partitioning by $X_1 \in I$ (using countable additivity over the countable state space $I$) and applying the Markov property at time $1$:
     $$\begin{aligned}
     h_i &= \mathbb{P}(\tau < \infty \mid X_0 = i) \\
         &= \sum_{j \in I} \mathbb{P}(X_1 = j \mid X_0 = i)\,\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\tau} < \infty \mid X_1 = j, X_0 = i) \\
         &= \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} h_j = (Ph)_i \geqslant 0
     \end{aligned}$$
     (with any $j$ having $p_{ij} = 0$ contributing $0$). In particular, $h_i \geqslant (Ph)_i \geqslant 0$.

Thus $g = h$ satisfies both (a) and (b).

---

### Part 2: (a) implies (c)

Suppose $g$ satisfies (a), so $g_i \geqslant (Pg)_i \geqslant 0$ for all $i \in I$, and in particular $g_i \geqslant 0$ for all $i \in I$. Recall
$$\tilde{p}_{ij} = \begin{cases} p_{ij} & (i \notin B), \\ \delta_{ij} & (i \in B). \end{cases}$$
Compute $(\tilde{P}g)_i = \sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{ij} g_j$:
- If $i \notin B$: $(\tilde{P}g)_i = \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} g_j = (Pg)_i \leqslant g_i$, by (a).
- If $i \in B$: $(\tilde{P}g)_i = \sum_{j \in I} \delta_{ij} g_j = g_i$, so $g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}g)_i$ (with equality).

Hence $g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}g)_i$ for all $i \in I$, which is (c).

---

### Part 3: Interpretation of $\tilde{P}$ and Minimality of $h$

**Interpretation of $\tilde{P}$.**
First note that $\tilde{P}$ is a stochastic matrix: $\tilde{p}_{ij} \geqslant 0$ for all $i, j \in I$, and $\sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{ij}$ equals $\sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} = 1$ for $i \notin B$ and $\sum_{j \in I} \delta_{ij} = 1$ for $i \in B$.

A Markov chain $\tilde{X} = (\tilde{X}_n)_{n \geqslant 0}$ with transition matrix $\tilde{P}$ evolves identically to $X$ while outside $B$, and is **absorbed (stopped)** once it enters $B$ (every state $i \in B$ is an absorbing state for $\tilde{P}$); equivalently, $\tilde{X}$ has the same law as the stopped chain $(X_{n \wedge \tau})_{n \geqslant 0}$. Consequently, for every $n \geqslant 0$ and $i \in I$, the event $\{\tilde{X}_n \in B\}$ occurs if and only if the original chain $X$ hits $B$ by time $n$, i.e.
$$\sum_{j \in B} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)} = \mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_n \in B \mid \tilde{X}_0 = i) = \mathbb{P}(\tau \leqslant n \mid X_0 = i). \tag{$\ast$}$$

*Rigorous verification of $(\ast)$ by induction on $n \geqslant 0$:* Set $r_i^{(n)} = \sum_{j \in B} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)}$ and $f_i^{(n)} = \mathbb{P}(\tau \leqslant n \mid X_0 = i)$.
- For $n = 0$: $\tilde{p}_{ij}^{(0)} = \delta_{ij}$, so $r_i^{(0)} = \mathbf{1}_{\{i \in B\}} = \mathbb{P}(\tau = 0 \mid X_0 = i) = f_i^{(0)}$.
- Assume $r_k^{(n)} = f_k^{(n)}$ for all $k \in I$.
  - If $i \in B$, then $\tilde{p}_{ik} = \delta_{ik}$, so $r_i^{(n+1)} = \sum_{k \in I} \tilde{p}_{ik} r_k^{(n)} = r_i^{(n)} = f_i^{(n)} = 1 = f_i^{(n+1)}$.
  - If $i \notin B$, by Chapman–Kolmogorov and Tonelli's theorem for sums of non-negative terms,
    $$r_i^{(n+1)} = \sum_{j \in B} \sum_{k \in I} \tilde{p}_{ik} \tilde{p}_{kj}^{(n)} = \sum_{k \in I} p_{ik} r_k^{(n)} = \sum_{k \in I} p_{ik} f_k^{(n)}.$$
    On the other hand, conditioning on $X_1$ and using the Markov property for $X$,
    $$f_i^{(n+1)} = \mathbb{P}(\tau \leqslant n + 1 \mid X_0 = i) = \sum_{k \in I} p_{ik}\,\mathbb{P}(\tau \leqslant n \mid X_0 = k) = \sum_{k \in I} p_{ik} f_k^{(n)}.$$
  Thus $r_i^{(n+1)} = f_i^{(n+1)}$ for all $i \in I$, proving $(\ast)$.

**Proof that $h$ is the minimal solution to (a), (b).**
Let $g$ be any solution to (a) and (b). As noted at the start, (a) implies $g_j \geqslant 0$ for all $j \in I$, and Part 2 gives $g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}g)_i$ for all $i \in I$.

We prove by induction on $n \geqslant 0$ that
$$g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}^n g)_i \equiv \sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)} g_j \qquad \forall i \in I.$$
- Base case $n = 0$: $(\tilde{P}^0 g)_i = g_i \leqslant g_i$.
- Inductive step: assume $g_k \geqslant \sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{kj}^{(n)} g_j$ for all $k \in I$. Since $\tilde{p}_{ik} \geqslant 0$, $\tilde{p}_{kj}^{(n)} \geqslant 0$, and $g_j \geqslant 0$, we may multiply by $\tilde{p}_{ik}$, sum over $k \in I$, and interchange the order of summation of non-negative terms (Tonelli's theorem):
  $$g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}g)_i = \sum_{k \in I} \tilde{p}_{ik} g_k \geqslant \sum_{k \in I} \tilde{p}_{ik} \sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{kj}^{(n)} g_j = \sum_{j \in I} \left(\sum_{k \in I} \tilde{p}_{ik} \tilde{p}_{kj}^{(n)}\right) g_j = \sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n+1)} g_j = (\tilde{P}^{n+1} g)_i.$$
*(Notice that no boundedness assumption on $g$ is needed here; non-negativity of $g$ and $\tilde{P}$ suffices.)*

Now drop the non-negative terms with $j \notin B$ and use (b) ($g_j = 1$ for $j \in B$) together with $(\ast)$:
$$g_i \geqslant \sum_{j \in I} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)} g_j \geqslant \sum_{j \in B} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)} g_j = \sum_{j \in B} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)} = \mathbb{P}(\tau \leqslant n \mid X_0 = i) \qquad \forall n \geqslant 0.$$
Since $\{\tau \leqslant n\} \uparrow \bigcup_{n=0}^\infty \{\tau \leqslant n\} = \{\tau < \infty\}$ as $n \to \infty$, continuity of probability from below gives
$$g_i \geqslant \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(\tau \leqslant n \mid X_0 = i) = \mathbb{P}(\tau < \infty \mid X_0 = i) = h_i \qquad \forall i \in I.$$
Since $h$ itself satisfies (a) and (b) by Part 1, $h$ is the minimal solution to (a), (b).

---

### Part 4: Irreducible $P$ is Recurrent iff Only Constant Functions Satisfy (a)

Note first that every non-negative constant function $g_i \equiv c$ ($c \geqslant 0$) satisfies (a), since $(Pg)_i = \sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} c = c = g_i \geqslant 0$ (while negative constants fail $(Pg)_i \geqslant 0$).

**($\Leftarrow$) Suppose every solution to (a) is constant. Prove $P$ is recurrent.**
Fix any state $b \in I$ and set $B = \{b\}$ (non-empty). Define $\tau_b = \inf\{n \geqslant 0 : X_n = b\}$ and $h_i = \mathbb{P}(\tau_b < \infty \mid X_0 = i)$. By Part 1, $h$ satisfies (a) and $h_b = 1$. By hypothesis, $h$ must be constant, so
$$h_j = h_b = 1 \qquad \forall j \in I.$$
Let $T_b = \inf\{n \geqslant 1 : X_n = b\}$ be the first return time to $b$. Conditioning on $X_1$ starting from $X_0 = b$ and applying the Markov property at time $1$ (noting that on $\{X_0 = b\}$, $T_b = 1 + \tilde{\tau}_b$ where $\tilde{\tau}_b = \inf\{m \geqslant 0 : X_{m+1} = b\}$):
$$\mathbb{P}(T_b < \infty \mid X_0 = b) = \sum_{j \in I} p_{bj}\,\mathbb{P}(\tau_b < \infty \mid X_0 = j) = \sum_{j \in I} p_{bj} h_j = \sum_{j \in I} p_{bj} \cdot 1 = 1.$$
Hence state $b$ is recurrent. Since $P$ is irreducible and recurrence is a class property, $P$ is recurrent.

**($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $P$ is irreducible and recurrent. Prove every solution to (a) is constant.**
First we show that for any fixed $b \in I$, the hitting probability $h_i^{(b)} = \mathbb{P}(\tau_b < \infty \mid X_0 = i)$ equals $1$ for all $i \in I$:
- For $i = b$, $h_b^{(b)} = 1$.
- For $i \neq b$, by irreducibility there exists a finite path $b = i_0, i_1, \ldots, i_m = i$ ($m \geqslant 1$) with $p_{i_0 i_1} p_{i_1 i_2} \cdots p_{i_{m-1} i_m} > 0$. By choosing a shortest such path (or taking the segment after the last visit to $b$), we may assume $i_1, \ldots, i_m \neq b$. Then the event $\{X_1 = i_1, \ldots, X_m = i_m, \; X_n \neq b \text{ for all } n \geqslant m\}$ is contained in $\{T_b = \infty\}$. Applying the Markov property at time $m$,
  $$\mathbb{P}(T_b = \infty \mid X_0 = b) \geqslant p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{m-1} i_m} \cdot \mathbb{P}(\tau_b = \infty \mid X_0 = i) = p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{m-1} i_m}\bigl(1 - h_i^{(b)}\bigr).$$
  Since $b$ is recurrent, the left-hand side is $0$. Because $p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{m-1} i_m} > 0$, we conclude $1 - h_i^{(b)} = 0$, i.e. $h_i^{(b)} = 1$.

Now let $g$ be any solution to (a) (so $g_i \geqslant (Pg)_i \geqslant 0$ and $g_i \geqslant 0$ for all $i \in I$). Fix an arbitrary state $b \in I$:
- **Case 1: $g_b > 0$.** Define $\hat{g}_i = g_i / g_b$ for all $i \in I$. Dividing (a) by $g_b > 0$ gives
  $$\hat{g}_i \geqslant (P\hat{g})_i \geqslant 0 \qquad \forall i \in I,$$
  so $\hat{g}$ satisfies (a), and $\hat{g}_b = 1$, so $\hat{g}$ satisfies (b) for $B = \{b\}$. By the minimality result of Part 3,
  $$\hat{g}_i \geqslant h_i^{(b)} = 1 \qquad \forall i \in I,$$
  and multiplying by $g_b > 0$ gives $g_i \geqslant g_b$ for all $i \in I$.
- **Case 2: $g_b = 0$.** Since $g$ satisfies (a), $g_i \geqslant 0 = g_b$ holds automatically for all $i \in I$.

Thus $g_i \geqslant g_b$ for all $i, b \in I$. Interchanging $i$ and $b$ yields $g_b \geqslant g_i$, so $g_i = g_b$ for all $i, b \in I$. Hence $g$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

## Final Answers

- $g = h$ satisfies (a) $g_i \geqslant (Pg)_i \geqslant 0$ ($\forall i$) and (b) $g_i = 1$ ($\forall i \in B$), with equality in (a) for $i \notin B$ and possible strict inequality for $i \in B$.
- Any solution $g$ to (a) satisfies (c) $g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}g)_i$ ($\forall i$), where $\tilde{P}$ is the transition matrix of the chain $X$ absorbed (stopped) on hitting $B$.
- Iterating (c) gives $g_i \geqslant (\tilde{P}^n g)_i \geqslant \sum_{j \in B} \tilde{p}_{ij}^{(n)} = \mathbb{P}_i(\tau \leqslant n) \uparrow h_i$, proving $h$ is the pointwise minimal solution to (a), (b) without any boundedness assumption.
- For an irreducible chain $P$, $P$ is recurrent if and only if the only solutions to (a) are (non-negative) constant functions.

## Confidence & Edge-Case Notes

- **Subtleties / Phase B watch-items:**
  1. **Non-negativity of $g$:** Condition (a) is $g_i \geqslant (Pg)_i \geqslant 0$, which automatically implies $g_i \geqslant 0$ for all $i \in I$. This non-negativity is what justifies Tonelli's theorem when interchanging sums in $\tilde{P}(\tilde{P}^n g) = \tilde{P}^{n+1} g$ and dropping the $j \notin B$ terms, with **no** boundedness assumption on $g$ (indeed, as our check script illustrates, solutions to (a) and (b) can take values strictly greater than $1$ outside $B$).
  2. **Infinite state space in Part 4 ($\Rightarrow$):** A classic pitfall is to try to prove $g$ is constant by taking a state $i_0$ that attains $\min_i g_i$ and arguing via $g_{i_0} \geqslant \sum_j p_{i_0 j} g_j$. That fails on an infinite state space because a non-negative function need not attain its infimum! Using the scaling $\hat{g} = g/g_b$ (when $g_b > 0$) and applying Part 3 with $B = \{b\}$ avoids taking any minimum/infimum and works seamlessly on arbitrary countable $I$ (without requiring $g$ to be bounded, either).
  3. **Distinguishing $\tau_b$ ($n \geqslant 0$) from $T_b$ ($n \geqslant 1$) in Part 4 ($\Leftarrow$):** Since $\tau_b = 0$ when $X_0 = b$, $h_b = 1$ always holds even for transient states! One must connect $h \equiv 1$ to the first *return* time via $\mathbb{P}_b(T_b < \infty) = \sum_j p_{bj} h_j = (Ph)_b = 1$.
  4. **Proving $h_i^{(b)} = 1$ for all $i$ when $P$ is irreducible and recurrent:** This should be justified cleanly rather than asserted without explanation; the shortest-path argument gives a 4-line self-contained proof.
