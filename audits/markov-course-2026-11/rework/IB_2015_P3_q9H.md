### Part 1: Definitions of communicating class and closed class

Let $(X_n : n \ge 0)$ be a Markov chain on a state space $S$ with transition matrix $P = (p_{ij})_{i,j\in S}$.

- We say state $i$ **leads to** state $j$, written $i \to j$, if there exists an integer $n \ge 0$ such that $(P^n)_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i(X_n = j) > 0$ (equivalently, $\mathbb{P}_i(X_n = j \text{ for some } n \ge 0) > 0$). In particular, $i \to i$ always holds by taking $n = 0$, since $(P^0)_{ii} = 1$.
- We say states $i$ and $j$ **communicate**, written $i \leftrightarrow j$, if both $i \to j$ and $j \to i$.
  (This is an equivalence relation on $S$: reflexive since $n=0$; symmetric by definition; and transitive because if $(P^m)_{ij} > 0$ and $(P^n)_{jk} > 0$, then $(P^{m+n})_{ik} \ge (P^m)_{ij}(P^n)_{jk} > 0$.)
- A **communicating class** is an equivalence class of $S$ under the communication relation $\leftrightarrow$.
- A subset (in particular, a communicating class) $C \subseteq S$ is **closed** if no state in $C$ leads to any state outside $C$:
  \[
  i \in C \text{ and } i \to j \implies j \in C.
  \]
  (Equivalently, $p_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \in C$ and $j \notin C$, or $\sum_{j \in C} p_{ij} = 1$ for all $i \in C$: clearly the definition implies $p_{ij} = 0$ for $j \notin C$ via $n=1$, and conversely $p_{ij}=0$ for $i\in C, j\notin C$ gives $(P^n)_{ij}=0$ for all $n \ge 0$ by induction on $n$.)

### Part 2: Communicating classes and closedness for $P$

The transition matrix on $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ is
\[
P = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\
0 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\
\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\
\frac{1}{2} & 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{2}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The positive one-step transitions are:
- from $1$: $1 \to 1$ and $1 \to 3$ (each probability $\frac{1}{2}$);
- from $2$: $2 \to 2$ and $2 \to 4$ (each probability $\frac{1}{2}$);
- from $3$: $3 \to 1$ and $3 \to 3$ (each probability $\frac{1}{2}$);
- from $4$: $4 \to 1$ and $4 \to 4$ (each probability $\frac{1}{2}$).

Determining reachability between all pairs:
- States $1$ and $3$ satisfy $1 \to 3$ and $3 \to 1$ (so $1 \leftrightarrow 3$), and $p_{12}=p_{14}=p_{32}=p_{34}=0$, so neither $1$ nor $3$ leads to $2$ or $4$.
- State $4$ leads to $1$ ($p_{41} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$) and hence to $3$ ($4 \to 1 \to 3$), and $p_{42}=0$, so $4$ does not lead to $2$. Since neither $1$ nor $3$ leads to $4$, state $4$ communicates only with itself.
- State $2$ leads to $4$ ($p_{24} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$), and hence $2 \to 4 \to 1 \to 3$, so $2$ leads to every state; however, column $2$ has $p_{12}=p_{32}=p_{42}=0$, so no state in $\{1,3,4\}$ leads to $2$. Thus state $2$ communicates only with itself.

Therefore, the **communicating classes** are:
1. $\{1, 3\}$ — **closed** (since $p_{12}=p_{14}=p_{32}=p_{34}=0$, no transition leaves $\{1,3\}$).
2. $\{4\}$ — **not closed** (since $p_{41} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$ and $1 \notin \{4\}$).
3. $\{2\}$ — **not closed** (since $p_{24} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$ and $4 \notin \{2\}$).

### Part 3: Distribution and expectation of $N$, and evolution of the chain from $X_0 = 2$

Given $X_0 = 2$, let $N = \inf\{n \ge 1 : X_n \ne 2\}$.

**Distribution of $N$:**
For each $n \in \{1, 2, \ldots\}$, the event $\{N = n\}$ is
\[
\{N = n\} = \{X_1 = 2, X_2 = 2, \ldots, X_{n-1} = 2, X_n \ne 2\}.
\]
From state $2$, the chain remains in $2$ with probability $p_{22} = \frac{1}{2}$ and leaves $2$ (necessarily jumping to $4$) with probability $1 - p_{22} = p_{24} = \frac{1}{2}$. By the Markov property (given $X_0 = 2$):
\[
\mathbb{P}(N = n) = (p_{22})^{n-1}(1 - p_{22}) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = 2^{-n}, \qquad n = 1, 2, \ldots
\]
(Note that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{-n} = 1$, so $N < \infty$ almost surely; $N \sim \mathrm{Geom}(\frac{1}{2})$ on $\{1,2,\ldots\}$.)

**Expectation $\mathbb{E}(N)$:**
Using the power-series identity $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n x^n = \frac{x}{(1-x)^2}$ for $|x| < 1$ (valid by term-by-term differentiation of $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n = (1-x)^{-1}$ inside its radius of convergence) with $x = \frac{1}{2}$:
\[
\mathbb{E}(N) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = \frac{1/2}{(1 - 1/2)^2} = \frac{1/2}{1/4} = 2.
\]
*(Alternatively, $\mathbb{P}(N > k) = 2^{-k}$ for $k \ge 0$, so $\mathbb{E}(N) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} 2^{-k} = 2$.)*

**Evolution of the chain when $X_0 = 2$:**
The evolution proceeds through three distinct stages (almost surely):

1. **Holding in transient state $2$**: Starting at $X_0 = 2$, the chain remains at state $2$ for $N - 1$ steps ($X_0 = X_1 = \cdots = X_{N-1} = 2$), where $N \sim \mathrm{Geom}(\frac{1}{2})$ on $\{1, 2, \ldots\}$ with mean $2$. At time $N$, the chain leaves state $2$ and **must jump to state $4$** ($X_N = 4$ a.s., since $p_{24} = \frac{1}{2}$ is the only transition out of $2$). Once it leaves $2$, it never returns to $2$.

2. **Holding in transient state $4$**: Let $M = \inf\{m \ge 1 : X_{N+m} \ne 4\}$. Since $N$ is a finite stopping time with $X_N = 4$, by the **strong Markov property** the post-$N$ process $(X_{N+k})_{k \ge 0}$ is independent of $(X_0, \ldots, X_N)$ (and hence $M$ is independent of $N$) and evolves as the chain started from state $4$. Since $p_{44} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $p_{41} = \frac{1}{2}$, $M$ is also distributed as $\mathrm{Geom}(\frac{1}{2})$ on $\{1, 2, \ldots\}$ ($\mathbb{P}(M = m) = 2^{-m}$, $\mathbb{E}(M) = 2$). The chain stays in state $4$ for $M - 1$ steps ($X_N = \cdots = X_{N+M-1} = 4$), and at time $T = N + M$ (with $\mathbb{P}(T = t) = (t-1)2^{-t}$ for $t \ge 2$ and $\mathbb{E}(T) = 4$) it **must jump to state $1$** ($X_T = 1$ a.s., since $p_{41} = \frac{1}{2}$ is the only transition out of $4$). It never returns to $4$ or $2$.

3. **Absorption into the closed class $\{1, 3\}$**: Once the chain enters state $1$ at time $T = N + M$, it remains in the closed communicating class $\{1, 3\}$ forever. Notice that rows $1$ and $3$ of $P$ are identical: $(\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{1}{2}, 0)$. Hence, after entering at $X_T = 1$, at every subsequent step $t = T+1, T+2, \ldots$ the next state $X_t$ is chosen uniformly from $\{1, 3\}$ (probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each), independently of the entire past $(X_0, \ldots, X_{t-1})$! That is, $(X_{T+1}, X_{T+2}, \ldots)$ are i.i.d. uniform on $\{1, 3\}$, independent of $(N, M)$.

*(Exact $n$-step distribution as a summary)*: For every $n \ge 1$,
\[
\mathbb{P}_2(X_n = 2) = 2^{-n}, \quad
\mathbb{P}_2(X_n = 4) = n 2^{-n}, \quad
\mathbb{P}_2(X_n = 1) = \frac{1}{2} - 2^{-n}, \quad
\mathbb{P}_2(X_n = 3) = \frac{1}{2} - n 2^{-n},
\]
which converges as $n \to \infty$ to the unique invariant distribution $\pi = \left(\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$.
